What Website Redesign SEO Protects

Website redesign SEO is the work of carrying your existing rankings through a rebuild. Search engines attach ranking history to specific page addresses, so a redesign that changes those addresses without a plan hands the history back and starts the climb over. The plan is a full inventory of what ranks now, a mapped destination for every old address, and redirects tested before the old site goes dark.

That inventory comes out of accounts you already own: the Pages report and the URL Inspection tool in Search Console, your analytics, the old sitemap.xml, and the internal links pointing at pages you may have forgotten you published. Anything holding impressions, clicks, or a link from another website goes on the list, and everything on the list gets a destination.

The links report is the part people skip. A page with three links from other sites carries value a fresh address has to earn from scratch, and those pages are often old ones nobody on the team thinks about anymore: a resource page, a press release, an article from six years ago. Losing the address loses whatever those links were doing for the whole site.

Then the content has to survive the redesign. A page ranks because of what's on it, so a rewrite that cuts the section carrying the ranking gives up the ranking too. Trimming for a cleaner look is fine on pages earning nothing. On the pages carrying your search traffic, the words stay and the new design goes around them.

Where a Redesign Loses Rankings

Old Addresses All Pointed at the Home Page

A blanket redirect from every old URL to the home page reads to Google as a soft error. The page that ranked has lost its content and the home page gained nothing relevant, so the ranking goes. Each old address needs the closest page that replaced it, one at a time, with a 301.

A Staging Site Left Open, or a Live Site Left Blocked

The noindex tag and the robots.txt rule that keep a half-built site out of search have to come off at launch, and the staging copy has to stay out of the index. This failure is the quiet one: the site looks perfect and Google's under instructions to ignore it.

Slower Than the Site It Replaced

A hero video, an uncompressed image set, and a fresh tag manager container can undo the load speed of the site you're replacing. Largest Contentful Paint, Interaction to Next Paint, and Cumulative Layout Shift are worth reading in PageSpeed Insights while the build is still sitting on staging.

Internal Links Still Aimed at the Old Addresses

Redirects catch the visitor, and a site full of internal links pointing through them wastes a hop on every click and muddies what the new structure is telling Google. Menus, footers, buttons, and links buried mid-paragraph all get rewritten to the new addresses, with the redirects left in place for the outside world.

The Form That Posts Nowhere

This one costs money fastest and has nothing to do with rankings. A contact form submitting to a dead address, or an old tracking number left in the header, means the traffic still arrives and the leads quietly stop. Every form gets submitted and every phone link gets dialed on launch day.

Who Needs Website Redesign SEO, and Who Doesn't

Four situations call for it: a site that already earns search traffic is being rebuilt, the business is moving to a different platform, the domain is changing, or two sites are being merged into one. A fifth is recovery, where the rebuild already happened and the traffic fell afterward.

Skip it when there's nothing to protect. A brand new business, or a site that has never had a visitor arrive from a search, has no ranking history to carry, and that money does more inside a build structured correctly from the start. Our web design page covers that route.

Recovery is its own case. When traffic dropped after a relaunch several months back, the skipped redirects, the deleted content, and the indexing problems can usually be found and corrected, and rankings often come back. They come back slower than they'd have held, which is the whole argument for doing this before a launch instead of after one.

On cost, the search side of a redesign gets scoped as a project or folded into monthly work, depending on what follows the launch. The build it wraps around costs $500 to $10,000+ depending on page count and who writes the copy, and ranges by builder sit on our website cost page.

What the First 90 Days After a Redesign Look Like

Launch week is a checklist, and it runs inside a day:

  1. Crawl the new site. Every internal link followed, every 404 found, every redirect chain shortened to a single hop.
  2. Spot check the old addresses. The list from the inventory, requested one at a time, confirming each one lands on the page it was mapped to.
  3. Submit and watch. The new sitemap filed in Search Console, then the Pages report read daily for the crawl and indexing errors that only appear once Google has been through.
  4. Confirm the tracking. Analytics firing, conversions recording, forms and phone links tested from an actual phone.

Whoever built the site does that work from a sheet we hand over: old address, new address, redirect type, and a column to check off once it resolves. Handing a developer a specific list beats asking them to remember what the old site had on it.

The two months after that are mostly watching. Rankings wobble following a migration even when the work was done correctly, and the pattern is what matters: a broad dip that recovers is normal settling, while one page that drops and stays down usually points at a mapping or content problem still worth fixing. After that the account joins the ordinary month, with launch day standing as the baseline every later report gets held against.

Frequently asked questions

Will a website redesign hurt my rankings?

It can, and when it does the cause is usually redirects that got skipped or content that got cut. A redesign planned around the pages that already rank usually holds them, and sometimes improves them once speed and structure get better.

Can you work with the designer we already hired?

Yes. We handle the URL inventory, the redirect map, the content parity check, and the launch-week testing, then hand the designer or the developer a specific list. That's a common way this gets scoped.

We already relaunched and traffic dropped. Can it be recovered?

Usually a good portion of it. We compare the old address list against the new site, find what returns a 404 or lands on the wrong page, and check whether the content that used to rank still exists anywhere. Recovery takes longer than prevention would have.

Do we have to keep the old URLs?

Keep them where there's no real reason to change them, since an address that stays put keeps its history with no redirect in between. When the new structure genuinely improves things, changing them is fine as long as every old address is mapped and redirected.

How long after a migration do rankings settle?

Expect a few weeks of movement while Google recrawls the site, and up to a couple of months on a large one. Watching which pages move and which stay down is what separates normal settling from a problem worth fixing.

Approve the Redesign With Its Rankings Still Attached

Two lists are worth holding before the new site goes live: what currently ranks on the old one, and where each of those addresses is headed. Both get built out of your own Search Console and handed to whoever is doing the build, ours or somebody else's.
