What a Rate Well Below the Range Buys

Flat fee offers that sit far under the bottom of those bands are easy to find, and it's worth knowing what the money buys before you answer one. Monthly labor costs what it costs: research, writing, technical fixes. A price that far under the published range has to save that money somewhere, and usually it saves it on automated listing blasts and copy nobody wrote for your business specifically. That work fills a monthly report while the ranking sits still.

The other tell is a promised outcome attached to the low price. No vendor controls how Google ranks anything, so a low fee paired with a confident promise about where you'll land deserves the most scrutiny in the stack, and the promise is the part to ask about first.

How to Read a Quote Before You Sign Anything

The number at the top of a proposal tells you less than the specifics underneath it. Before you set two prices side by side, ask which pages or keywords get worked this month, who does the writing, and how results get measured against where you started. A vendor answering in generalities is describing activity, and a vendor answering in specifics is describing a plan.

  • Ask which pages, and which term each one targets. A quote naming URLs and the term behind each is describing scoped work. A quote that says "content included" is still guessing.
  • Ask for the rank history itself. A vendor confident in its own numbers will show you one. We keep ours public, on our results page, for exactly that reason.
  • Ask what you keep if you leave. Your Google Business Profile and your site access should transfer to you whether or not the engagement continues.
  • Ask whether the price accounts for your town. We look at what the competition actually looks like in a market like Dalton or Cedartown before scoping anything, because a national rate card treats every Georgia zip code like a metro.

Frequently asked questions

Are these your prices?

No. The ranges above are published 2026 market figures gathered from broader industry pricing data, not a menu of what we charge. What your own engagement costs depends on your market, the condition of your current site, and how many keywords or towns are in scope. It becomes a specific number after a conversation about your business.

Does the price change if my business already has a Google Business Profile set up?

Somewhat. A profile with some history, clean categories, and no suspension flags means less cleanup up front, but it rarely changes which range you land in, since the ongoing research, writing, and citation work costs the same either way. A messy profile adds work.

What happens to the price if I need SEO for more than one location?

Each additional location adds its own Google Business Profile, its own citation set, and usually its own page, so a multi location engagement scales up from the single location ranges. Two locations in neighboring towns often cost less to add than two locations far apart, since some of the research and content work overlaps.

Will the price go down once my rankings are established?

Not usually, though the work shifts. Early months lean on research, citation cleanup, and new pages. Once a ranking holds, that same budget goes toward defending the spot and adding new keywords, because a competitor fills whatever gap you leave.

Should I choose a monthly retainer or a one time project?

Pick the retainer if your trade or town is contested, since a ranking there needs new content and upkeep just to hold the spot it already won. Pick the one time project if the job has an end: a technical cleanup, or a fresh batch of pages against a set deadline, priced at a project rate against the cleanup end of the one time row above.
