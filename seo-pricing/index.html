Why There Is No Single Answer to 'What Does SEO Cost'

There is no single price for SEO. There is a published range, and there are a handful of facts about your business that decide where inside it you land. Search for seo pricing and dozens of pages will promise you one clean number anyway. A trade competing in one small town needs a smaller scope than a specialty practice pulling customers from a wider radius in a busier market. Ten target keywords cost less to research, build, and track than a hundred. A site with clean technical bones takes less work to rank than one dragging years of unresolved problems behind it. A business starting from zero visibility needs different work than one defending a spot it already holds.

What follows are published 2026 market ranges, gathered from broader industry pricing data rather than pulled from our own invoices, so you have real numbers to hold any proposal against. Which end of a band your own engagement lands on comes down to your market, your competitors, and the site you already have. Our website cost breakdown covers the site itself, if that is what you are pricing rather than the SEO behind it.

What a Rate Well Below the Range Actually Buys

Flat fee offers that sit far under the bottom of the local band are easy to find. It is worth knowing what that money buys before you answer one. Real monthly labor costs what it costs: research, writing, technical fixes. A price that far under the published range has to save that money somewhere, and usually it saves it on automated listing blasts and copy nobody wrote for your business specifically. That work fills a monthly report without moving a ranking.

The other tell is a promised outcome attached to that low price. No vendor controls how Google ranks anything, so a low fee paired with a confident promise about where you will land deserves the most scrutiny, not the least. A number that low is not hidden efficiency. It is a sign the labor above is not happening at the scale a real ranking effort takes.

How to Read a Quote Before You Sign Anything

The number at the top of a proposal tells you less than the specifics underneath it. Before comparing prices, ask what pages or keywords are actually being worked on this month, who is doing the writing, and how results get measured against where you started. A vendor who cannot answer in specifics is describing activity, not a plan.

  • Ask for the actual pages, not a page count. A quote naming real URLs and the specific term each one targets is describing scoped work; a quote that just says "content included" is still guessing.
  • Ask to see it, not hear about it. A vendor confident in its own numbers will show a rank history rather than describe one. We keep ours public, on our results page, for exactly that reason.
  • Ask what you keep if you leave. Your Google Business Profile and site access should transfer to you whether or not the engagement continues.
  • Ask whether the price accounts for your actual town. We look at the real competition in a market like Dalton or Cedartown before scoping anything, instead of pricing off a national assumption that treats every Georgia zip code like a metro.

Frequently asked questions

Are these your prices?

No. The ranges above are published 2026 market figures gathered from broader industry pricing data, not a menu of what we charge. What your own engagement costs depends on your market, the condition of your current site, and how many keywords or towns are in scope. That becomes a real number only after a conversation about your business.

Does the price change if my business already has a Google Business Profile set up?

Somewhat. A profile with real history, clean categories, and no suspension flags means less cleanup up front, but it rarely changes which range you land in, since the ongoing research, writing, and citation work costs the same either way. A messy profile adds work rather than removing it.

What happens to the price if I need SEO for more than one location?

Each additional location adds its own Google Business Profile, its own citation set, and usually its own page, so a multi location engagement scales up from the single location ranges rather than sitting inside them. Two locations in neighboring towns often cost less to add than two locations far apart, since some of the research and content work overlaps.

Will the price go down once my rankings are established?

Not usually, though the work shifts. Early months lean on research, citation cleanup, and new pages. Once a ranking holds, that same budget goes toward defending the spot and adding new keywords instead of shrinking, because a competitor fills whatever gap you leave.

Should I choose a monthly retainer or a one time project?

Pick the retainer if your trade or town is genuinely contested, since a ranking there needs new content and upkeep just to hold the spot it already won. Pick the one time project if the job has an end: a technical cleanup, or a fresh batch of pages against a set deadline, priced closer to the audit and cleanup range above.
