Why Is My Google Business Profile Not Showing Up?

The profile exists but is not appearing where it should, so here are the likeliest causes and what to check first.

Get a free visibility audit

What You're Dealing With

This page is about a profile you know exists. Maybe it showed up fine last year. Maybe you can see it in your own dashboard right now. It is just not appearing in Maps or Search for the searches that should bring it up, or it has quietly dropped out of the map pack it used to hold.

A Different Animal From an Invisible Website

A dropped map pack spot and an invisible website are different problems with different fixes. A plumber's website can rank nowhere for "plumber" and the phone still rings all day, because the Business Profile holds the map pack on its own. A fast, well built site can sit unseen locally because nobody ever filled out the profile behind it. If the website itself is the real issue here, the more useful guide is why isn't my website showing up on Google. If there is no profile yet to speak of, start with how to get your business on Google.

Two Places to Look, and the Order to Look in Them

Some causes show up the moment you search from outside. Others only appear once you are logged into the dashboard. Working in that order saves a step.

Visible From Outside the Account

  • Plain competitive loss. Nothing is broken. The profile is live and being outranked by competitors with a more complete profile, a stronger category match, or more recent reviews. Put your listing side by side with whoever holds the spot now and this one shows up fast.
  • A gap in service area coverage. Search from a neighboring town the business covers; if nothing turns up there, the towns list on the profile is too narrow or was never filled in.
  • A duplicate quietly splitting the identity. Search the business name plus the city; a second, older, or auto generated listing competing with the real one is visible the moment it appears in that search.

Visible Only From the Dashboard

  • A suspended status. A rules review, frequently triggered by an edit to the name, address, or category, can take a profile down without much visible warning outside the account itself.
  • A verification step stuck in limbo. An edit or a video check still pending review holds a profile back from showing at all, and the dashboard is the only place that says so.
  • A category quietly mismatched to how people search. Two categories with almost the same name can rank for entirely different searches. Catching it means opening the dashboard and comparing your own category against what a competitor winning that search has selected.

Check These Yourself First

  1. Search your business name in a signed out or private window. Your own login biases what Google shows you, so a signed-out search is the only reliable way to see the listing a customer sees.
  2. Check your Business Profile dashboard directly for a status flag. A suspended or pending verification status is usually spelled out there, rather than left for you to guess at from the outside.
  3. Review and update your service areas. List the towns and radius you serve rather than leaving this blank or untouched since setup.
  4. Reconsider your primary category against two or three close alternatives. Look at what similar businesses currently outranking you have listed, and make sure your own choice matches how customers search, not just how the business describes itself internally.
  5. Search your business name plus your city to spot a duplicate. If a second, older, or auto generated listing turns up, note it, it will need to be reported or merged rather than ignored.
  6. Add a few current photos and check your posting history. A profile that has not been touched in months signals to both Google and a searcher that the business may not be active, even when it very much is.

When to Bring In Help

Checking a status flag, updating service areas, and reconsidering a category are things most owners can do themselves in under an hour. Suspensions and duplicates are a different, more technical category of problem, and worth treating that way rather than guessing at a fix.

Three cases are worth handing off:

  • A suspension. Start with the notice itself, because the appeal has to answer the exact reason it names. Reinstatement runs on evidence. Business documents, consistent name and address data, proof the business operates as described. A rejected first attempt makes the next review harder, not easier.
  • A duplicate that needs a formal merge. Merging incorrectly can lose reviews that took years to accumulate.
  • A straight competitive gap. The profile is fine. It is being outworked by a competitor posting, updating photos, and collecting reviews on a schedule yours has not kept up with, which needs sustained upkeep rather than a single repair. Frequently asked questions

Should I just delete the profile and start over if it won't show up?

No, and it is worth stopping on this one. Recreating a profile that already has history throws away reviews and trust signals that took years to build, and starting fresh rarely fixes whatever was wrong in the first place. Diagnose the specific cause first; a brand new listing is closer to a last resort than a first move.

Google wants a video walkthrough before it will verify us. Is that normal?

Yes, and it has become more common, particularly for businesses without a public storefront. Paperwork and signage go a long way toward proving a business exists on camera; a vehicle or the equipment itself rounds it out for trades where that applies. Polish is not the point, evidence is.

How many service areas can I list?

Google caps the list at a set number of towns or counties. List the ones the business serves rather than guessing at a smaller or larger footprint than reality.

Can I speed up a suspended profile's reinstatement?

Not by resubmitting the same appeal repeatedly. A single, complete filing with the right supporting documents tends to move faster than several rushed, incomplete ones.

Does a low review count explain a missing profile?

It can be a contributing factor, since review count and recency are part of what Google weighs, but it is rarely the only cause. Category, service areas, and status are worth checking first.
