What a Homeowner Types When the Foundation Moves

The first search is a description, never a diagnosis. Stair step crack in brick. Door that won't latch anymore. Sloping floor in the back bedroom. Gap opening above the garage door. Musty smell under the house. Water in the basement after every hard rain. Those phrases come from somebody standing in a room looking at the problem, and they carry more intent than any service term this trade owns.

The vocabulary changes about an hour into the reading. Now it's piers and underpinning, wall anchors, slab lifting, crawl space encapsulation, French drain, sump pump. Then the two questions that end the session: what the repair runs, and whether insurance covers any of it.

Most sites in this trade lead with the method list, in the words the crew uses, so the top of the search never reaches them. Our keyword research work separates the two vocabularies.

Google sorts local results by where the searcher stands, and this trade covers ground a plumber never would. Three Categories a Foundation Repair Listing Could Sit In

General contractor is where a lot of these listings end up. A pier crew sitting there competes with kitchen remodelers while the structural searches go elsewhere. Concrete contractor reads as flatwork to Google and to everybody clicking, so the calls arrive about driveways and patios. Waterproofing service is right for a company whose revenue is water, drainage and encapsulation and sump systems, and wrong for an outfit whose money is in structural lifting.

The primary belongs to whichever half pays the bills. Secondary slots pick up the sidelines: Excavating contractor for the company that also digs, Masonry contractor for the crew that rebuilds brick after a lift. Structural engineer names a licensed profession, so it belongs only to a firm employing one. Our free audit names which of these three a listing sits in today.

Whether to Show the Address on a Foundation Repair Listing

The work happens at somebody's house, so hiding the address is an option Google allows. This trade sells a warranty measured in decades, and a homeowner spending that kind of money looks for evidence the company will exist to honor it. A yard on a county road reads as permanence.

Red Clay, Limestone, and When Foundation Calls Cluster

Georgia's red clay takes on water slowly and gives it up slowly, moving while it does either one. A dry summer pulls it back from a footing, the rain returns in the fall, and the movement turns up at the seams of the house a season later. Northwest Georgia adds a second story, since the valley floors of Floyd, Bartow, Gordon, and Chattooga counties sit on limestone, where water travels through rock and the problems read differently.

Construction type shifts the vocabulary too. Slab and crawl space houses make up most of the housing stock here, with full basements mainly on hillside lots, so basement waterproofing carries search weight in some counties and almost none in others.

Demand arrives on a lag. A dry stretch produces cracking calls months later, and the first hard rain after it produces water calls inside two days. So the drainage pages get worked while the ground is dry, and the structural pages get theirs while the phone is busy with water.

A Foundation Repair Review Arrives a Year Late

This trade gets the hardest review ask in the building trades. The day the crew finishes, the yard is torn up, the patch concrete looks like patch concrete, and the homeowner just wrote the biggest check of the year for something nobody will see.

Three moments do produce reviews. Backfill and cleanup day, if the yard genuinely got put back, since that's the detail customers write about more than the engineering. A few weeks out, after the first heavy rain, when the drain did what it was sold to do and the money finally feels well spent. And the warranty check visit, a standing appointment this trade already schedules and rarely uses for anything else.

Reviewers here write about three things. Whether the estimate held. Whether the crew put the yard back. Whether the phone got answered after the check cleared, the quiet fear behind every bad review here.

Bad ones often come from a sales visit instead of a job, and a short reply with a name and a direct line reads well nine months later.

Photographs earn their place: a pier going in, a wall before and after with the crack closed. Our review management work builds those three ask moments into the calendar.

Frequently asked questions

Which Google category should a foundation repair company use?

General contractor pulls remodel and addition searches, Concrete contractor pulls driveway and patio calls, and Waterproofing service fits a company whose revenue comes from drainage and crawl space work. Match the primary to where the money actually is, then use secondary slots for genuine sidelines such as excavating or masonry.

Why should a foundation repair site have pages about cracks instead of piers?

Because homeowners describe what they see before they learn what it is called. Stair step cracking in brick, a door that won't latch, a sloped floor, and water in a crawl space after rain are the phrases people type first, and a site organized around method names never meets any of them.

When does a page about a stair step crack start bringing in calls?

A symptom page such as stair step crack in brick wall usually needs several months to settle into position in a county the crew already works, and then it keeps producing for years. Category and service area corrections on the listing show up much sooner than that.

How does a foundation repair company collect reviews when the job leaves a torn up yard?

Ask at the moments the customer feels good about it. Backfill and cleanup day if the yard got put back properly, a few weeks later after the first heavy rain proved the drainage, and at the warranty check visit. Those three produce the specific detail careful buyers read for.

Do real estate closings change what a foundation repair website needs?

They add an audience with a deadline. A buyer or seller working against a closing date wants an evaluation this week, a written scope, a repair a lender will accept, and a clear answer on whether the warranty transfers. Pages covering those four convert that traffic quickly, and agent and inspector pages bring more of it.
