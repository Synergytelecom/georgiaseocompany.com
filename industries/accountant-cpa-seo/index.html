Tax season decides most of this trade's search year, and the work that wins January happens in October.

What People Type Before They Hire an Accountant

Almost nobody searches for accounting. They search for the thing sitting on the kitchen table: a tax preparer nearby, somebody who handles an S corp return, a bookkeeper who knows QuickBooks, help with a letter from the IRS, three years of returns that never got filed. The wording tells you which of two very different buyers is on the other end of it.

One is a household filing once a year, comparing on price and on how fast they can get an appointment. The other is a business owner looking for someone to hold the books, run payroll, and pick up the phone in June. That second relationship lasts years and pays every month, and it starts from a search worded around a specialty.

What the Accountant Map Pack Rewards

Distance does much of the sorting on a nearby search, and then the three cards get picked on how complete each profile looks and how recently anybody reviewed it. Two things carry extra weight in this trade.

Hours have to reflect the season, because a firm keeping Saturday appointments from February through April and never saying so on the profile loses the calls those hours were meant to catch.

And the national tax chains hold storefronts across Georgia, with review counts built up over years, so a local firm chasing the household return is climbing past that pile.

Accountant, Certified Public Accountant, or Tax Preparation Service?

Accountant is the general primary, and it suits a firm doing tax work, books, and advisory under one roof. Certified public accountant is the closer match for a licensed CPA, and the license behind it is public record with the Georgia State Board of Accountancy. Tax preparation service belongs to a seasonal preparer whose work really is returns, and it's the mismatch worth checking first, since a year-round advisory firm sitting under it gets aimed at the once-a-year price shopper and stays invisible to the client it wants.

Bookkeeping service, Payroll service, Tax consultant and Business management consultant earn secondary slots when the firm genuinely sells those lines. A slot spent on something the firm barely touches waters down the primary.

One Firm, One Verified Location

Small practices tend to accumulate listings. A partner creates one, an office manager creates another at a suite number that changed years ago, and a seasonal storefront leaves a third behind.

Google reads all three as competing records of the same place, reviews split across them, and pack position goes to whoever has one clean listing. The free audit lists every listing carrying the firm's phone number and what each one's set to.

A practice run out of a home office has a decision to make here. Verification is built around a real place, so the address either shows publicly or the firm runs as a service area business with it hidden.

Hiding it gives up some proximity weight in the map pack, and most owners would rather pay that than put a house on a map.

Where Accounting Firm Websites Fall Short

A lot of accounting sites run on a vendor package: a tax-tips library, a monthly newsletter, a client portal button, and a stock photograph of a calculator. The library is the problem.

Those same articles publish under hundreds of firm names, so Google has no reason to prefer one copy, and the firm stays invisible for the towns it works in while its blog quietly fills up.

What's missing is the part that pays. A page for bookkeeping. A page for payroll.

A page for entity setup and the S corp election question every new owner asks. A page for representation when a notice arrives. And a page for each industry the firm actually knows, because contractors, restaurants, trucking companies, and medical practices all search by their own trade, and a firm that says construction bookkeeping out loud gets found by builders.

The staff page matters more here than in most trades. A client is about to hand over bank access and a Social Security number, so names, credentials, license status, and a real photograph of each person carry weight a stock boardroom shot never will. Those pages get written during the web design work, at the same time as the search structure.

Where an Accounting Practice Earns Its Reviews

The natural review moment here is weak. A client drops off a folder, gets a finished return, pays the invoice, and feels a relief nobody thinks to write down.

So the reviews have to be asked for, and the timing's the whole trick. The ask lands when the work gets handed over, in the meeting or in the email carrying the finished return, while the client still remembers what it took.

Two cautions come with the territory. Reviews that praise the size of a refund attract people shopping for a number, and the number was never the firm's to promise.

And a public reply confirming somebody's filing situation discusses a client's business in front of everybody, so replies stay short and general. Asking steadily across the year also keeps the profile from looking dormant from June to December, which is what happens when every review on it carries an April date.

The First 90 Days of Accountant SEO

The calendar decides the order. A firm starting in the fall has time to be ready for January. A firm starting in February works the profile and the reviews through the season, then builds in the spring.

  • The listing, first. Recover or claim it, set the primary and fill the secondary lines, clear the duplicates left behind by a partner or an old suite number, put the season hours in with Saturday coverage spelled out, and load photographs of the office and the people in it. The name, address, and phone get matched across the records Google reconciles a listing against.
  • Then the pages that sell the year-round work. Bookkeeping, payroll, entity setup, notice representation, and a page for each industry the firm can back up in a first meeting. The contact path gets cut down so a business owner can say what they need in three fields.
  • Then the routine. The review ask goes into document handover, profile posts get a schedule, the first town pages go up, and every term gets measured against where it began.

What CPA SEO Reports Month to Month

An accounting year has five deadlines in it, and the month's work is set by whichever one sits closest. January through mid April is the household wave, and nothing structural gets built during it. The two extension deadlines in September and October bring their own searching, from business owners who ran out of runway.

Quarterly estimate weeks produce a small reliable bump. November and December are the year-end planning conversations, which is the highest-value searching this trade sees and the least contested. The pages for each of those windows get written in the quiet stretch ahead of it.

The report keeps the two halves of the practice apart, because a firm ranking well for tax prep and missing on bookkeeping has a page problem, while a firm missing on both has a profile problem, and those get fixed in different places. Profile calls and direction requests get counted beside the positions, since a listing collecting views and no calls says something a ranking chart hides. A firm shopping agencies in the fall can read what the accounts under management rank for, on the results page.

Frequently asked questions

Should a licensed CPA use the Accountant category or Certified public accountant?

Certified public accountant while the license is current, since it matches the search somebody runs when they specifically want a CPA. Accountant works as the primary for a firm that mixes tax, books, and advisory without leading on the license, and the other lines belong in the secondary slots either way.

Can an accounting practice run from a home office and still rank locally?

Yes, with a tradeoff. Verification runs on a real location, so a home-based practice either publishes the address or hides it and works as a service area business. Hiding it gives up some proximity weight in the map pack, and most owners would rather pay that than put a home address in front of the public.

Does the tax-tips newsletter that comes with an accounting website help rankings?

Rarely. The same articles publish under hundreds of firm names, so there's nothing about one copy for Google to prefer. Pages written about the services the firm sells and the industries it knows do the work that library was supposed to do.

Is it worth building a page for each industry an accounting firm serves?

Yes, for the industries the firm genuinely knows. Business owners search by their own trade, so a page about restaurant accounting or trucking bookkeeping answers a question a general services page leaves sitting there. Write only the ones you can back up in a first meeting.

When should an accounting firm start SEO if it wants results by tax season?

Autumn. Profile work can move a listing within weeks, but service pages, reviews, and citation cleanup need a few months of running before January demand arrives. A firm that starts in February should spend the season on the profile and the review routine, then build in the spring.

When a January Envelope Sends Somebody Looking for a CPA

Those envelopes reach Georgia mailboxes in the same two weeks every year, and a good share of the people opening them will type a search before the weekend. Whoever comes up first already had the category set, the pages built, and a few reviews with recent dates on them.
