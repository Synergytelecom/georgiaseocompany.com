What Photography Clients Type Before They Book

A couple hunting for a wedding photographer types the town or the venue along with it. A parent looking for senior portraits types the high school. Newborn photographer, family photos, corporate headshots, real estate photography, product shots for a shop that just started selling online: every one of those is a different buyer with a different budget, and a single portfolio page tries to answer all of them at once.

Lead times run from a year to an afternoon. A couple usually starts looking nine to eighteen months out. An expecting parent books a newborn session during the third trimester. Senior portraits get scheduled the summer before the school year. A headshot gets searched and booked inside a week, because somebody took a new job and needs a current photo by Friday.

What the Photography Map Pack Rewards

Proximity sorts the three cards first, which is how a photographer two towns over ends up above better work. After that it's the category, the review count, and how finished the profile looks. Photos carry unusual weight here for an obvious reason: the buyer is hiring somebody to make images, so a dozen current photographs from real sessions say more than any paragraph could.

The Photographer Category Call on Google

Google keeps the genres apart. Photographer is the broad one. Wedding photographer, Portrait studio, Photography studio, Commercial photographer and Video production service each sit separately in the list, and each one pulls a different search.

The primary follows the revenue. A business earning most of its income from weddings belongs under Wedding photographer, because that's the phrase a couple types. A studio clients walk into for portraits fits Portrait studio or Photography studio. A photographer working out of a home office with no client visits stays under Photographer, with the address hidden and a service area that covers only the counties they'll actually drive to.

The temptation is to claim every genre in the secondary slots. A slot spent on work the business barely does waters down the primary, and a genre page with no images behind it disappoints whoever clicks it. What Photographer Websites Get Wrong

This trade builds some of the prettiest sites in local search and some of the hardest ones for Google to read. Four problems repeat.

  • The site is images with almost no words. A full screen slideshow and a contact link give a search engine nothing to match against what somebody typed.
  • The galleries live somewhere else. Client galleries hosted on a third party platform sit on that platform's domain, so the traffic and the text around them build up a site the photographer will never own.
  • The images are enormous. A page of untouched exports takes seconds to paint on a phone, and a visitor on a slow connection leaves first.
  • One portfolio page covers six genres. A bride, a real estate agent, and an executive who needs a headshot all land in the same grid and have to sort themselves out.

The fix is dull and it works. A page per genre on your own domain with real sentences on it. Venue and location pages naming the places you shoot most. Images exported at web sizes with file names and alt text. A starting price range, so the inquiries arriving come from people who can pay for the work. That structure belongs in the web design stage, before the first gallery goes up.

Review Dynamics in a Photography Business

The moment worth asking is delivery. A shoot is a good day, and the gallery landing in an inbox two weeks later is when a client sees what they paid for and feels something about it. A review request attached to that email gets answered. The same request sent a month afterward mostly gets ignored.

Volume works against weddings. A couple hires one wedding photographer in their life, so wedding work produces a single review per client and no second one. Portraits, headshots, and family sessions repeat, and they're where a review count actually grows. Real estate agents are the account worth chasing on purpose, because an agent shooting listings every month turns into a standing client and a referral source to other agents.

This trade also has a habit of collecting praise where Google can't see it. Testimonials pasted into the site, comments under a social post, and tagged stories all feel like proof, and a map ranking only counts what sits on the profile.

The Seasons a Georgia Photographer Books Against

Fall is the crowded stretch. October and November carry weddings, family sessions, and holiday card work at the same time, and that calendar fills during the spring before it. Spring runs second, with weddings and graduations. Summer belongs to senior portraits, since the sitting happens the summer ahead of the senior year. Corporate headshots cluster in January and after a rebrand or a hiring push.

Some towns carry a second graduation season. Rome has Berry College and Shorter University, Carrollton has the University of West Georgia, and Kennesaw State sits in Kennesaw, so campus portrait demand lands in those markets on the school's calendar.

Demand arrives months ahead of the shoot, and a page takes months to settle into a ranking. That timing is the argument for working the site in January and February, while the calendar is quiet.

The First 90 Days of Photographer SEO

  • The profile, verified and honest. Primary category matched to the genre that pays, address hidden if clients never visit, service area set to the counties you will genuinely drive to, and hours handled the way Google handles appointment work, meaning no main hours and a booking link standing in their place. A real photo set loaded from recent sessions, and the name, address, and phone made consistent across the outside records that corroborate a listing.
  • Genre pages, on your own domain. One per genre, images resized and named, alt text written, starting ranges published, and a page for each venue or location you shoot most often.
  • Reach, then routine. Town pages for the communities clients drive in from, the public portfolio moved onto the site where it can earn traffic, and the review request written into the gallery delivery email.

After that the shooting calendar sets the schedule, and it runs about six months ahead of itself. Wedding inquiries for next fall arrive this spring, so the wedding and venue pages get worked in winter. Senior portrait searching lands in May and June for sittings that happen in July. Holiday card and family session demand shows up in September. Headshot work spikes in January and whenever a large employer in the county starts hiring. Each of those gets attention in the quarter before it, and the report shows which genres and which towns moved. Frequently asked questions

Wedding photographer or Photographer as the primary Google category?

Follow the revenue. If weddings pay most of the bills, Wedding photographer matches the phrase a couple types and belongs in the primary slot, with Photographer filled in as a secondary. A generalist shooting portraits, events, and commercial work is better off with Photographer as the primary and the specific genres around it.

Should a photographer working from home show the address on Google?

Hide it when clients never come to you. The listing runs as a service area business, and Google still measures distance from where the business really sits, so your home base decides which towns are within reach either way. That's why the service area has to be honest about the drive.

Do client galleries hosted on another platform help a photography website rank?

They help the platform. Galleries served from a third party domain build traffic and authority for that domain, so the public portfolio work belongs on your own site. Private delivery galleries can stay wherever they're convenient, since nobody is searching for those.

When is the right moment to ask a photography client for a review?

At gallery delivery. The images landing in an inbox is when the client actually sees the work, and a request in that email with a direct link to the profile gets answered far more often than one sent weeks later.

Should a photographer publish prices on the website?

A starting range, yes. Photography inquiries collapse when a visitor has no idea whether the work is within reach, and a starting number tells serious clients they're in the right place. The full price list can stay in the guide you send after the inquiry comes in.
