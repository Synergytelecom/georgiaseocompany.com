Restaurant Searches Start With the Cuisine and the Drive

Diners type a cuisine and a place. Barbecue near me, Mexican restaurant with the town attached, best wings, brunch open now. Cuisine is the word carrying the search, and place is how far somebody will drive for it on a Tuesday.

Then the decision happens on the map card, with no click involved. Photos, star rating, price level, hours, the menu link, the order and reserve buttons, how busy the room is right this minute, and the drive time. A restaurant's website is often the fourth or fifth thing a diner looks at, and plenty of them never get that far.

Open now is a filter, so hours behave like a ranking factor here. Holiday hours are the ones nobody sets, which is how a restaurant that opened on the Fourth of July vanishes from the search that day while three closed competitors show up in its place.

Underneath the pack, the organic results belong to Yelp, TripAdvisor, the delivery apps, and the best restaurants in town roundups. One restaurant holds its own name, its cuisine paired with its town, the dishes it's known for, and the occasion searches, which is plenty to work with.

The Restaurant Category Is the Cuisine, Not the Word Restaurant

Google keeps a category for the cuisine. Barbecue restaurant, Mexican restaurant, Southern restaurant, Pizza restaurant, Seafood restaurant, Steak house, Breakfast restaurant, Coffee shop, Sports bar. A generic Restaurant primary competes for a word few people type and gives up the cuisine pack where the real searches sit.

Additional slots take whatever else is genuinely sold. Caterer is the valuable one, since catering is a separate search with a much bigger ticket behind it. Takeout, delivery, and bar categories go in the same way, when they're real.

Attributes are the other half of the card. Dine in, takeout, delivery, outdoor seating, good for kids, accepts reservations, wheelchair accessible entrance. Those feed the filters diners actually tap. The ordering button deserves a look too, because it often points at an aggregator taking a cut of every ticket until the owner sets a link of their own.

A PDF Menu Costs a Restaurant the Dish Searches

A menu posted as a PDF or a photograph is a download. The dish names inside it answer no search for those dishes, and on a phone the thing pinches and scrolls while a hungry person gives up on it. The menu belongs on a page as text, with sections a thumb can move through and item names written as headings, and the profile's menu link pointed at that page.

Photos work the same way. Customer photos usually outnumber the owner's, and the owner's job is making sure the first images Google shows are the food and the room. A logo sitting in the first slot spends the lead image on the card saying nothing about dinner.

Restaurant Reviews Age Faster Than Most Trades Do

Diners review restaurants without being asked, in both directions, so volume is seldom the problem. Recency is. A glowing review from two years ago describes a kitchen with different staff in it, and a rough month after a chef change shows up on the card within days. Steady arrivals and short, plain owner replies are what keep the page current.

Past the menu, the pages worth building are catering, private events and large parties, and the room itself. Those searches carry the biggest tickets a restaurant sells, and no delivery app answers them.

The First Ninety Days for a Georgia Restaurant

Month one is the card. Cuisine category, attributes, hours set out through the holidays, ordering and reservation links pointed where the owner wants them, and a photo set that leads with food.

Month two is the menu and the money pages. The menu goes up as text, then catering, private dining, and large party pages, each answering the questions a planner asks before calling.

Month three is the market. Where the cuisine searches rank in town and in the towns nearby, what the profile produced in calls, direction requests, and website taps, and the first report.

Then it runs monthly. Georgia hands a restaurant a rhythm to work with. Sunday lunch after church, Friday nights in the fall when a high school game empties one side of town and fills the other, event weekends on Broad Street in Rome and in downtown Cartersville, travelers coming off I-75 at Calhoun and Adairsville looking for something better than a drive through. Frequently asked questions

Does a restaurant with a busy Google profile still need a website?

Yes, for the things the card has no room to hold. The menu as readable text, catering and private event pages, the story of the room, and a destination for the menu link on the profile itself.

Is a PDF menu really a problem?

It is. Dish names locked in a PDF can answer no search for those dishes, and on a phone a PDF turns into a pinch and scroll job that loses hungry people. Text on a page fixes both at once.

How should a restaurant with two locations handle Google profiles?

One profile per location, each with its own address, hours, photos, and menu link. A second location folded into the first listing competes properly for neither town.
