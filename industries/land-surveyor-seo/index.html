Why Land Surveyors Rank on Referrals More Than Reviews

Almost nobody searches for a land surveyor twice in the same year, which changes where the work goes.

Most Clients Need You Once and Never Again

Most of the trades on this site sell something a customer might need again within a year: another cleanout, another trim, another repair. A land survey is usually a once-and-done purchase tied to a specific transaction, a boundary dispute, a new construction permit, a subdivision plat, or a title closing, and the same client may not need one again for a decade, if ever.

Two Numbers Only You Have

Both of the numbers that decide this sit in your own books. The first is what you charge for a simple boundary survey against what a full ALTA or topographic survey bills at, two price tiers rather than two points on the same scale. The second is how much of the work on your calendar right now came from an attorney, an agent, or a builder rather than from someone who found you cold. That second one decides how much weight search should carry at all. Field time and drafting time set how many surveys a crew finishes in a month, which is a hiring question long before it is a marketing one.

A calendar kept full by referrals makes a modest ranking cheap to live with. It is the quiet stretch, when the referrals thin out, that a page showing up cold has to cover.

New Lots and Permits Are What Generate Surveys

New construction and subdivision activity, not raw population, is what drives survey demand, since every new lot line, easement, and building permit needs one before anything gets built. That makes the county permit office a better read on next quarter than any population table. Bartow County, home to Cartersville and its roughly 25,000 residents, sits on the I-75 corridor, where that work takes its usual form: boundary lines for new lots, staking for new construction, plats for new subdivisions. Gordon County, around Calhoun, and the flooring and manufacturing corridor running toward Dalton carry the same mix of commercial and residential work behind them, even where it is less visible from the highway. Commercial lending increasingly requires an ALTA survey specifically, not just a boundary survey, before closing, which adds another source of demand tied to business activity rather than residential construction alone.

Your License Number Does What Photos Do Elsewhere

There is only one category that fits: Land surveyor, with no second option to weigh it against. Photos carry less weight here than they do for a tree service or a roofer, because nobody hires a surveyor based on a picture of fieldwork. What earns trust is a visible license number, your stamp and seal on a sample document, and service descriptions specific enough that a title attorney recognizes what they are reading. Reviews come in slowly here, which is what a low job count does. You close a limited number of jobs a year, so a handful of reviews tied to specific closings and permit projects counts for a lot. The only thing that adds more of them is more closings.

Organize the service pages by survey type rather than by city. Boundary surveys, ALTA and ACSM surveys, topographic surveys, and construction staking each answer a different professional's question, since the audience is often an attorney, an agent, or a builder searching for a specific service, not a homeowner searching a general term.

Frequently asked questions

How does a survey practice even know what share of its work is referral versus cold search?

By asking. Add 'how did you hear about us' to intake, write the answer down every time for a few months, and you will know. Memory is not reliable on this one, and the answer decides whether your budget should protect referral relationships or go after the cold searches they never reach.

If photos carry less weight, what should a survey practice's profile actually show?

A redacted sample plat or survey drawing tends to do more work than a photo of the fieldwork itself, since it demonstrates the deliverable a client or referring attorney is paying for. A short list of the specific survey types and counties covered helps too, since that is often the first thing a searcher is trying to confirm.

Who should a survey practice ask for a review: the client, or the attorney who referred them?

The end client, once the survey has been recorded or the transaction has closed. A referring attorney sending repeat business has less reason to leave a public review of a vendor they already use routinely, while a client asked right after closing, with the paperwork still fresh, tends to write something specific and credible.

Does a survey practice need city-by-city pages the way a same-day trade does?

Usually not to the same degree. A single service-area page listing the counties covered tends to outperform a full set of city pages, since this audience typically searches by survey type first and checks coverage second, the reverse order from a trade like junk removal or tree service.
