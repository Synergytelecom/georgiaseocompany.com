Why Land Surveyors Rank on Referrals More Than Reviews

Almost nobody searches for a land surveyor twice in the same year, which changes where the work goes.

Most Clients Need You Once and Never Again

Most of the trades on this site sell something a customer might need again within a year: another cleanout, another trim, another repair. A land survey is usually a once-and-done purchase tied to a specific transaction, a boundary dispute, a new construction permit, a subdivision plat, or a title closing, and the same client may not need one again for a decade.

The work also arrives sideways. Most of it comes through an attorney, an agent, or a builder who already knows you, so a calendar kept full by referrals makes a modest ranking cheap to live with. It's the quiet stretch, when the referrals thin out, that a page showing up cold has to cover. That stretch is the job search is doing in this trade, which is why the pages have to be built and ranking before you need them.

New Lots and Permits Are What Generate Surveys

New construction and subdivision activity drives survey demand more than raw population does, since every new lot line, easement, and building permit needs one before anything gets built. That makes the county permit office a better read on next quarter than any population table. Bartow County, home to Cartersville and its roughly 25,000 residents, sits on the I-75 corridor, where that work takes its usual form: boundary lines for new lots, staking for new construction, plats for new subdivisions. Gordon County, around Calhoun, and the flooring and manufacturing corridor running toward Dalton carry the same mix of commercial and residential work behind them, even where it's less visible from the highway. Commercial lending increasingly requires an ALTA survey specifically before closing, a source of demand tied to business activity more than to residential construction.

Your License Number Does What Photos Do Elsewhere

There's only one category that fits: Land surveyor, with no second option to weigh it against. Photos carry less weight here than they do for a tree service or a roofer, because nobody hires a surveyor based on a picture of fieldwork. What earns trust is a visible license number, your stamp and seal on a sample document, and service descriptions specific enough that a title attorney recognizes what they're reading.

Reviews come in slowly, which is what a low job count does. You close a limited number of jobs a year, so a handful of reviews tied to specific closings and permit projects counts for a lot. The only thing that adds more of them is more closings.

We build the service pages by survey type: boundary surveys, ALTA and ACSM surveys, topographic surveys, and construction staking. That audience is usually an attorney, an agent, or a builder searching for a specific service before they search for a firm, so the page that names the service is the page that gets found.

Frequently asked questions

How does a survey practice even know what share of its work is referral versus cold search?

By asking. Add 'how did you hear about us' to intake, write the answer down every time for a few months, and you will know. Memory is not reliable on this one, and the answer decides whether your budget should protect referral relationships or go after the cold searches they never reach.

If photos carry less weight, what should a survey practice's profile actually show?

A redacted sample plat or survey drawing does more work than a photo of the fieldwork itself, since it demonstrates the deliverable a client or referring attorney is paying for. A short list of the specific survey types and counties covered helps too, since that is often the first thing a searcher is trying to confirm.

Who should a survey practice ask for a review: the client, or the attorney who referred them?

The end client, once the survey has been recorded or the transaction has closed. A referring attorney sending repeat business has less reason to leave a public review of a vendor they already use routinely, while a client asked right after closing, with the paperwork still fresh, writes something specific and credible.

Does a survey practice need city-by-city pages the way a same-day trade does?

Usually not to the same degree. A single service-area page listing the counties covered outperforms a full set of city pages, since this audience typically searches by survey type first and checks coverage second, the reverse order from a trade like junk removal or tree service.
