Interior and Exterior Painting Are Two Different Sales

Interior work runs all year and sells on care. Furniture moved and covered, clean lines, a crew a homeowner can stand being around for four days, and a room usable again by the weekend. Exterior work is bound to the weather and sells on preparation. Scraping, caulking, priming bare wood, and a coating that still looks right after three Georgia summers.

Commercial and repaint contract work is a third track entirely. Property managers turning apartments, HOA boards on a repaint cycle, builders finishing new construction, and offices working around business hours. That buyer prices per unit, signs on a schedule, and cares about crew size and insurance more than color consultation.

Cabinet refinishing sits off to the side and deserves attention. It carries a much higher ticket per day of work, gets searched as its own thing, and the buyer is weighing it against tearing the cabinets out. A company doing that work well and burying it on a services list is leaving its best margin invisible.

The Category a Painting Contractor Belongs Under

Painter is the primary category for a painting contractor, and it's the name that matches what a homeowner types. Google carries a vaguer Painting category next to it, which covers the same ground with a looser label and gains a profile nothing on the searches worth ranking for.

Dry wall contractor and Pressure washing service earn secondary slots when a crew genuinely performs that work, since both get searched separately and both show up on real painting invoices. General contractor looks tempting and works against a painter, because it invites remodel and addition searches the company has to decline while watering down the painting terms that pay.

Google's list has no category for cabinet refinishing, so that side has to be spelled out in the service list and given its own page, with photographs of finished doors and drawer fronts behind it. What Painting Contractor Websites Get Wrong, and What Earns the Reviews

A Gallery With Nothing Written Around It

Photos are the product in this trade, and most painting sites post thirty finished rooms in a grid with no words attached to any of them. A project entry that names the town, the surface, the problem, the number of coats, and the product line gives a homeowner something to trust and gives a search engine something to read. It's the same photograph doing ten times the work.

Prep Described Where a Homeowner Reads It

Prep is what separates a paint job that holds from one that peels in two summers, and it's the part almost nobody explains on a website. A page that walks through the pressure washing, the scraping, the caulking, the spot priming, and what gets masked inside the house beats a competitor showing prettier pictures with no process behind them. Insurance, workers compensation coverage, and how the crew protects a floor belong there too.

An Estimate Path That Works at Ten at Night

A homeowner browsing after the kids go to bed will fill out a form and won't phone an office that's closed. An estimate request asking for room count, surfaces, and a couple of photos captures that person and gives the estimator enough to plan the visit. A phone number by itself loses them to whoever made it easy.

Reviews on a Job That Lives in the House for a Week

The crew is in the home for days and talking with the owner the whole time, which makes the final walkthrough the natural moment to ask. Reviews in this trade name the crew lead, and that's worth encouraging, since a homeowner reading that the same two painters showed up every morning is reading the exact thing they were worried about. Interior jobs get reviewed more readily than exteriors, and a repaint cycle runs years, so the count grows off new customers and commercial accounts.

Frequently asked questions

Painter or Painting as the Google category for a painting contractor?

Painter. It reads the way a homeowner searches, and Painting is the looser label sitting beside it with no extra reach to offer a contractor. Dry wall contractor and Pressure washing service make sense as secondaries when the crew actually does that work.

Should interior and exterior painting have separate pages?

Yes. They're different seasons, different buyers, and different worries. An interior customer is thinking about disruption in their house, an exterior customer is thinking about durability and prep, and one page written for both ends up shallow on each.

Does describing prep work on a painting website really help?

It helps more than another gallery row. Prep decides whether the finish lasts, homeowners have usually heard that from somebody, and hardly any painting site explains it. A page that walks through washing, scraping, caulking, priming, and masking answers the question a careful buyer is already asking.

Do lead marketplace listings hurt a painting contractor's own visibility?

They crowd the page under the map pack. Those sites rank nationally for painting phrases and resell the same homeowner to several contractors, so the practical answer is to hold a map pack seat and a real page for each town you serve, which is traffic nobody resells.

When should a painting contractor ask for a review?

At the final walkthrough, with the owner standing in the finished room and the crew lead present. The relationship is a week old at that point, the result is visible, and a request made face to face with a direct link gets answered far more often than an email sent after the invoice.
