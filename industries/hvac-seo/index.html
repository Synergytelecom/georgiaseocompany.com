HVAC contractor, or Air conditioning repair service?

All of this is checkable on your own screen in about ten minutes, which matters because we don't have an HVAC client. Nothing here is a result we produced.

  1. Open your Google Business Profile, tap Edit profile, then Business category.
  2. One Primary field sits on top, with room for nine more beneath it. Ten in total is Google's published cap.
  3. Type air conditioning into the Primary field. Back come Air conditioning contractor, Air conditioning repair service, Air conditioning system supplier and Air duct cleaning service. Clear it, type HVAC, and you get HVAC contractor.

Air conditioning repair service is the one picked by mistake, and it's an honest mistake, since it describes what the crew did every day last August. As a primary it aims the profile at the repair searcher. HVAC contractor is the primary for a company that installs and services both heating and cooling, and it's where the change-out shopper lands. A change-out (the trade's word for a full system replacement) is the biggest ticket on the board. Air conditioning contractor fits a cooling-focused shop that does little heating install or repair. Heating contractor, Furnace repair service and Air duct cleaning service belong in the nine additional slots. If nobody has opened that field since setup, a free audit finds it.

What a No-Cool Call Is Worth Against a Change-Out

Averaging the three job types produces a figure that describes none of them. Sort last August's invoices into three piles:

  • No-cool ticket. Won on the phone in under a minute, at a price almost nobody negotiates, and worth one ticket. Clusters in July and August, then again on the first hard freeze in January as a no-heat.
  • Change-out. Won after two or three quotes and a week of reading, at many times a repair ticket. Trails the heat by a month or two, since the system that limped through August gets replaced in September or October.
  • Planned maintenance agreement. Two small visits a year, sold ahead of the season, and the only one you can put on a calendar twelve months out.

That third one is worth more than its own revenue, because it's the list your change-outs come off. The technician who opens an aging condenser in April starts that conversation. Reviews split the same way: a customer whose house is cool again by dinner writes one unprompted, and a spring tune-up customer has to be asked, so the ask belongs inside the visit.

Replacement Shoppers Read Before They Call

Open Search Console, open the Performance report, and sort the query table by impressions. Queries carrying a model number, a SEER2 rating or a refrigerant name come from replacement shoppers weeks away from picking up the phone. Since January 1, 2023 the Department of Energy's minimum has been regional, and Georgia sits in the Southeast region, where a split-system central air conditioner under 45,000 BTU has to hit 14.3 SEER2. The AHRI reference number on each quote is checkable at ahridirectory.org.

Those readers want the general page split four ways: repair for the no-cool and no-heat calls, replacement and install for the shopper, maintenance agreements for the renewal, and system type, where heat pumps and the ductless mini-splits common in older houses with no ductwork get named. Duct sealing and attic insulation get bundled into a change-out often enough now to be worth naming there too.

Your Conditioned Air Contractor license number, Class I or Class II, is verifiable at verify.sos.ga.gov, and a Trane Comfort Specialist or Carrier Factory Authorized Dealer badge belongs beside a photo of your own technician on a real job site, truck in the frame.

Ringgold and Fort Oglethorpe Get the Same August

Several trades on this site rise and fall with new construction. HVAC demand sits about level across market sizes, because every occupied house in Georgia needs cooling through August. Ringgold, the Catoosa County seat, has 3,425 residents; Fort Oglethorpe, near the Tennessee state line, has just over 10,600. August makes no adjustment for either figure, and houses around both towns still run ductwork installed decades before current efficiency standards, which keeps duct replacement work coming in. New construction shifts the mix more than the total, installs in a growing suburb against replacement work in an older town.

Commercial changes the buyer. Rooftop package units sell to a property manager typing different words than a homeowner, under a commercial HVAC category with pages of its own.
