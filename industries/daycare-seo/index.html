How Parents Search for Child Care

The words split by age before they split by anything else. Infant daycare, toddler daycare, two year old program, preschool near me, pre k, after school program, summer camp. A parent with a nine month old and a parent with a rising kindergartner are running two different searches, and one center usually serves both.

The second split is need, and it is urgent. Daycare with openings, daycare that takes CAPS, second shift child care, drop in daycare, daycare open at six. Somebody typing one of those has a start date already circled and will call three centers in a row until a phone gets answered.

Proximity works differently here than it does for a trade that drives to the customer. The relevant distance is the drive from home to the center to work, so the radius bends along a commute.

A center sitting on a route into Rome or Cartersville reaches parents two towns out, while a center a mile off that route loses them, and that shows up plainly in which town pages earn visits.

Day Care Center, Preschool, or Both on the Listing

Day care center is the primary for most licensed centers. Preschool is a separate category with its own search behind it, and a center running a real curriculum for three and four year olds should hold that slot rather than hope the word gets read out of a description. After school program and Summer camp earn slots when those programs are real, since parents shop them separately from full time care. Child care agency describes a placement or nanny service, and it lands on center listings by accident more often than owners expect.

The license class shapes the wording too. Georgia licenses child care learning centers and family child care learning homes through Bright from the Start, and those are two different businesses with two different capacities. A home based program described in center language draws inquiries it has to turn away, and it starts every one of those calls with a correction.

Two state programs get searched by name, so they belong in the copy. Quality Rated stars are worth naming on the site and in the listing description once a center has earned them, because parents type the phrase.

Georgia's lottery funded Pre-K runs in private centers as well as public schools, and a center offering it deserves a page for it rather than a bullet on a services list. Our free audit reads back what the listing claims today, and profile management keeps the hours, photos, and program list current after that.

What a Child Care Website Has to Answer Before the Tour

The tour books the family, and a short list of questions stands between a search and a tour. Which rooms have openings and when the next one frees up. What tuition covers and how it changes by age. The earliest drop off and the latest pickup. Meals.

The potty training policy. What happens on a snow day and which holidays close the building. How the front door locks and who holds the code. What a teacher's day looks like, and how long the teachers have been there.

Most center websites answer three of those. The rest go to a phone call a working parent tries at lunch, and the parent who reached voicemail keeps scrolling.

A page per age program, a tuition page that explains the structure honestly, and a waitlist form that says what happens after somebody fills it out will do more for enrollment than any redesign.

Photographs are the hard part in this trade, since a child's face needs a signed release and plenty of families decline. The workaround is the rooms themselves, clean and honestly lit: the infant room, the cots, the playground, the sign in desk, the kitchen. Our web design work puts those photographs on the program pages themselves, where a parent hunting for the infant room actually lands.

Where Daycare Reviews Come From, and When to Ask

The ask moments in child care sit at emotional high points, and there are more of them than most centers use. The end of a first month, once drop off has stopped being a scene. A conference where a teacher walks a parent through a portfolio.

The week a family leaves for kindergarten, which is the most sentimental day on the calendar. A family who called in a panic and got an opening the same week.

Teacher reviews matter here for a reason particular to this trade. Staffing caps enrollment, and every director in Georgia knows it. The listing a parent reads is also the listing a lead teacher reads before applying, so a review from somebody who has taught there four years is a hiring asset and a marketing asset in the same paragraph.

Replies stay short and never name a child or a room. A thank you, the director's first name, and a phone number covers it. Review management turns the asking and the answering into a routine somebody actually runs each month.

The First Quarter of Daycare SEO Work

First out is anything a parent uses to rule a center out: hours, the category set, the license wording, a phone number that rings at the desk, and photographs showing the rooms as they are. Then the program pages by age, then the openings and waitlist path, then pages for the towns along the commute the center actually draws from.

The rest waits on the center, and that is worth agreeing to early. Quality Rated material waits until a rating is in hand.

The review routine waits for a staff script and the director's blessing, because a request handed to a teacher without warning gets skipped. Summer camp pages want photographs from last summer, which means the camp page gets built in winter out of a folder somebody has to go find.

The enrollment year sets the pace after that. January brings the families who decided over the holidays, spring brings the camp searching, and August brings the after school scramble, so a page has to be finished a season ahead of the search that needs it.

Infant rooms book furthest out and deserve the earliest attention. Every account we run has its positions published where anybody can read them, dated and unnamed, on the results page, which is the straightest answer available to a director who has already been pitched twice this year.

Frequently asked questions

Does a preschool need a different Google category than a daycare?

Often yes. Day care center and Preschool draw two different sets of parents typing two different things, so a center with a genuine curriculum for the threes and fours should claim both, with the primary matched to the bulk of its enrollment. After school program and Summer camp deserve slots when those programs genuinely run.

What should a child care website say about openings?

Say which rooms have space and how the waitlist works, and update it. Openings are the first thing a parent screens for, and a site that stays silent sends the call to the center down the road. A waitlist form that explains what happens next converts the parents you have no room for today.

Can a center collect reviews without posting photos of children?

Yes. Reviews and photos are separate problems. Ask at the calm moments, a month after enrollment, at a conference, or the week a family moves up to kindergarten, and publish photographs of the rooms, the playground, and the staff instead of faces. Keep every reply free of a child name.

Where should a Quality Rated star rating show up online?

On the home page, on the program pages, in the listing description, and in the answer to any tuition question. Parents search the phrase directly, and a rating mentioned nowhere on the website is a credential the center paid for and never spent.
