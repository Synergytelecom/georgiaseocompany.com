What a New Client Types Before They Book a Salon

Two kinds of people search for a salon. One just moved and has to replace a stylist they trusted for nine years. The other had something go wrong somewhere else and needs it fixed this week. Neither searches the word salon by itself.

They search the service. Balayage, color correction, silk press, keratin treatment, extensions, a curly cut, a men's cut, a first haircut for a two year old, bridal hair. They search the texture too, which is where most Georgia salons leave money on the table, because curly, coily, and fine hair all get searched as specialties and hardly anybody claims one.

A salon competing on the word haircut is up against every chair in the county. One built for curly hair that says so, or whose colorists take corrections others turn down, is up against two or three.

The Open Now Filter Decides More Salon Searches Than Reviews Do

A lot of this searching happens in a car, between two other errands. Google filters those results by what's open, which makes hours part of whether a salon competes at all.

A salon closed Mondays with no hours set, or open until eight and showing six, drops out of the results people run. Holiday hours count the same way and get loaded in advance.

Hair Salon or Beauty Salon: the Category That Splits the Traffic

Hair salon is the primary for a business whose money comes out of the chair. Beauty salon is broader, reaching nails, lashes, and waxing, so a hair business sitting there competes with every service under the sun. Barber shop is a different search with a different expectation, and Hairdresser is its own category as well. Secondary slots go to what's really on the menu and staffed: Nail salon, Waxing hair removal service, Day spa.

Two profile fields go unused here more than in any other trade. Google lets a salon list its services, and most leave it empty while a menu with forty items hangs by the register.

Google also takes a booking link, which puts an appointment one tap from the search result while the salon's phone stays free for the chair. Before anything gets changed, the free audit lists the hours, the category, the services on the profile, and every record sharing the salon's street address.

A Salon Address With Five Listings on It

Booth rental makes this trade different. Stylists renting chairs are independent businesses, licensed by the state board the same as the salon, and several will eventually create their own Google listings at the salon's address. Nobody did anything wrong.

Google still reads a row of separate businesses at one street number as rivals for the same map pin. The reviews spread across all of them, and the salon's own record ends up thinner than its parts.

The arrangement that holds up is one listing for the salon, plus a website page for each stylist carrying her specialties, portfolio, and booking link. A renter who genuinely runs a separate business, with her own suite and hours, is a different case and gets handled that way on purpose. Either way it's a conversation worth having before six people verify six listings, and our Google Business Profile management is where that cleanup happens.

What a Salon Website Does That an Instagram Feed Can't

Instagram is where the work lives, and it should be. It has no interest in ranking for color correction in a Georgia town, though, and a client who found a salon on a grid still has to dig up hours, prices, and a way to book.

So the site carries the parts a feed skips, which is why our web design settles them before anybody picks a palette. The full menu by service with ranges, because a client who can't find pricing assumes the worst.

The policies nobody enjoys writing: cancellation windows, deposits on long color services, patch tests, and what happens when a client arrives with an unmentioned box color.

Photographs shot in the salon's own light, because a client comparing two salons reads the walls and the chairs. And a booking flow that works with one thumb.

Salon Reviews Get Asked at the Mirror

There's an exact second in every appointment when the chair turns and the client sees it. That's the peak of the whole relationship, and it happens dozens of times a day in a busy salon. Most of those clients walk out without anybody asking.

The ask belongs to the stylist, at the mirror, in her own words. A client rebooking at the desk is nearly as good, and so is the color correction that took two sessions and came out right, or the bride once the photos come back.

Clients name their stylist when they write, and those first names get searched. A stylist who changes salons takes that search with her, one more reason the stylist pages should carry the names the reviews do.

Replies stay short, warm, and signed by a person. The one that earns its keep answers a bad color review: no argument, one offer to make it right, a direct number. Every future client reading that thread is deciding whether the salon stands behind its work, and that outweighs the average.

Prom, Homecoming, and the Rest of the Salon Year

Few local trades have a calendar this predictable. Formal season fills the spring, homecoming fills two weeks in the fall, weddings run March into October, December turns into parties and gray coverage before family photos, and late August brings back to school haircuts by the hundred. January is the flat month, when everybody just spent their money and the chairs go quiet by two.

All of it is schedulable. The bridal and formal hair pages get finished ahead of the searching, and January is where the color education writing, the gift certificate page, and the new-client offers earn their place, because that's when a salon has time to convert somebody who was only browsing.

What SEO for Salons Changes in the First Season

Week one is unglamorous and moves the most. Hours corrected, holiday hours loaded, category set, service list filled in, booking link connected, real photographs uploaded. Those are same-week changes, and they decide whether the salon turns up in an open-now search at all.

The listings at the address get sorted next, and that takes longest, because it involves other people's businesses. Service pages and stylist pages come after, in the order of what the salon wants more of. The review count grows one chair turn at a time.

A salon owner choosing between three agencies is doing what a new client does with three salons, hunting for evidence somebody's work holds up. That evidence sits on the results page, current positions listed by trade.

Frequently asked questions

Should a hair salon pick Hair salon or Beauty salon as its primary category?

Hair salon, when the revenue comes out of the chair. Beauty salon is a broader category covering nails, lashes, and waxing, so a hair business sitting there gets sorted in with services it never sells. Keep Beauty salon, Nail salon, or Day spa in the secondary slots only when the salon really staffs those services.

Can booth renters and the salon all have Google listings at one address?

They can create them, and it usually costs everybody. A single street number carrying five verified businesses leaves Google no clean answer about which record belongs on the map, and the reviews land in five places instead of one, so the salon record loses weight. One listing for the salon plus a stylist page on the website for each renter holds up better, unless a renter genuinely operates a separate business with her own suite and hours.

Do salon hours really change whether the listing shows up?

They do, because a large share of salon searching happens on the move and gets filtered by what is open right now. Wrong hours, missing hours, or unset holiday hours take a salon out of results it would otherwise appear in, and no amount of content makes up for that.

Is an Instagram account enough, or does a salon need a website?

Instagram carries the portfolio and earns the follow. It has no way to rank for a service and a town, and it leaves every question between a follow and a booking unanswered. A site with the service menu, pricing ranges, stylist pages, deposit and cancellation policies, and a booking flow that works on a phone converts the people the feed attracts.

Which is worth more to a salon, hair salon near me or the service searches?

The service searches, because of what they book. Hair salon near me pulls whoever is nearest and cheapest, and every chair in the county is already standing in it. A balayage page or a color correction page brings in a client who sits three hours and comes back every eight weeks, which is worth a pile of walk-in haircuts. Own the specialty first and the crowded term matters less.
