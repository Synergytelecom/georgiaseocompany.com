A dealership runs three businesses off one address, and a single Google listing is usually trying to represent all three.

Three Dealership Searches: Inventory, Service, and Financing

Inventory searches carry the most volume and the least opportunity. A buyer typing a year, a make, and a model gets CarGurus, Autotrader, Cars.com, Carfax, and the manufacturer's own inventory tool, and one lot outranking that stack on a model search is a long project with a thin payoff.

Service searches run the other way. Oil change near me, brake repair, check engine light, tire rotation, transmission service. Those sit in a local pack a dealership can hold, and they bring the same customer back several times a year. Most stores leave them to the independent shop two blocks down.

Financing searches come from a buyer who has run out of patience with filters. Buy here pay here, first time buyer program, bad credit auto loan, what my trade is worth. Those searchers are close to a decision and they're hunting for a lot that says yes in plain words on a page.

The Brand Category and the Dealership Service Department Listing

A franchise store files the brand category. Ford dealer, Chevrolet dealer, Toyota dealer, whichever franchise is on the sign, because a brand loyal buyer types the brand and Google matches that to the specific category. A generic Car dealer primary gives the match away. An independent lot with no franchise usually fits Used car dealer, which is also closer to the words people use looking for one.

The bigger miss is the service department. Google supports a separate listing for a department with its own entrance, its own hours, and its own phone line, and a service drive qualifies on all three counts. One listing covering sales and service leaves the oil change pack with no dealership in it, and that pack is where the repeat money lives.

The main listing's additional slots carry the rest of what the store really runs. Auto repair, parts sales, truck sales, leasing, and financing each have a category, and each earns a slot only when the store genuinely does it. Hours matter more here than in most trades, since service closes hours before sales does, and a Saturday searcher who reads the wrong ones goes elsewhere without calling.

Dealership Inventory Pages Disappear When the Car Sells

The website vendor builds a page for every VIN on the lot, thousands over a year, and each one dies the day that vehicle sells. A site shedding hundreds of addresses a month teaches Google that most of what it holds is temporary.

Retired vehicle pages need somewhere durable to land, which means the durable pages have to exist first. Used trucks, three row SUVs, work vans, a page for each model the store keeps in stock, a page for the mileage and price bands buyers filter on. Those accumulate authority while the VIN pages come and go underneath them.

Ownership is the question to settle before any of that. A dealer on a vendor platform often can't edit the template, doesn't hold the content, and hasn't checked who the domain is registered to since the last website change. That answer decides what's possible.

Georgia buyers drive for the right vehicle. A store in Rome, Calhoun, or Cartersville gets shopped against inventory in Atlanta and across the line in Chattanooga, so the town list runs out to the distance somebody would drive for the truck they want, a wider radius than a home services business would ever draw.

Dealership Reviews Come From the Finance Office and the Service Lane

One rough finance experience produces a long, specific, one star review that sits on the profile for years and can't be answered away. Volume is the only real defense, and the volume is there, because a store touches hundreds of customers a month once the service drive is counted.

Service is where most of it should come from. A sales customer appears once every several years and a service customer appears three times a year, so the review routine belongs at the cashier window in the service lane as much as at the sales desk.

Two habits work against that. Manufacturer and third party review programs collect into their own sites, which builds a score the map pack never reads, so the ask has to name Google. And a CRM set to request reviews only from customers who scored well on an internal survey is filtering, which Google's policy prohibits and which produces a wall of unbroken five stars that reads false to the shopper scrolling it.

What Dealership SEO Does in the First Ninety Days

The first pass is an inventory of a different kind. Who owns the domain, what the vendor platform will let anybody edit, what the primary category is set to today, whether the service department qualifies for a listing of its own, and what the hours claim for each department. Photographs of the actual lot and the actual bays replace the stock renderings.

The durable pages come next, since everything else on a dealer site is temporary by design. Service by job, financing by situation, the model and body style landing pages, and redirects so a sold vehicle's address still lands somewhere useful.

Then it widens. Town pages across the drive radius, plus a page on Georgia tag and title, because the one time title ad valorem tax on a vehicle purchase is a question buyers raise on nearly every deal and almost no lot answers on its website.

After that the store's own calendar runs the month. Refund season from February into April is when a down payment shows up in a buyer's account, and the financing pages have to be placed before the first check clears. Model year changeover moves the used inventory pages. The first cold snap fills the service drive with batteries and no-starts. Year-end sales events deserve a page that already exists in October rather than one built the week of. The report keeps each department separate, town by town. Every account we hold has its positions published on the results page.

Frequently asked questions

Should a dealership service department have its own Google Business Profile?

Yes, when the department has its own entrance, its own hours, and its own phone number. That listing is how a store competes for oil change and brake repair searches, which the sales listing was never positioned to win.

Does the manufacturer national site compete with a franchise dealer in search?

On brand terms, constantly. The store wins on local pairings: brand plus town, service plus town, and the financing and trade in questions the corporate site hands back to the dealer anyway.

What should happen to a vehicle page after the car sells?

Point it at a page that lasts, usually the model page or the body style page it belonged to. Leaving thousands of dead vehicle addresses behind is a slow drag on every other page the site has.

Can a dealership ask only its satisfied customers for reviews?

No. Screening requests by an internal survey score runs against Google policy, and it backfires anyway, since a page of unbroken five stars reads as managed to the exact shopper it was meant to convince.
