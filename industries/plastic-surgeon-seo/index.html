What a Plastic Surgery Patient Searches Before the Consult

Elective surgery gets researched the way people research a car or a mortgage. The searches start broad and get narrower over a month or two: the procedure name, then the procedure plus a city, then cost, then recovery time, then a specific surgeon's name once two or three finalists exist. Rhinoplasty, breast augmentation, tummy tuck, eyelid surgery, and the combination procedures patients name themselves all behave this way.

Cost is the modifier that shows up most and the one practices answer least. Somebody typing a procedure and the word cost is not tire-kicking; they're trying to find out whether to keep reading. A page that gives a starting range, explains what moves the number, and says plainly what financing exists keeps that person on the site.

The Second Search, After the Consult

Patients also search a surgeon's name after meeting them, looking for board certification, hospital privileges, complaints, and other people's results. What Google shows for that name search is part of the decision, which makes the bio page and the profile as load-bearing as any procedure page.

What the Map Pack Rewards in Plastic Surgery

Plastic surgeon near me is settled largely on distance and profile strength, and a practice can win its own town and county without much argument. The metro-wide procedure terms are another matter, because the practices holding those results have been collecting reviews and building procedure pages for years, and paid ads sit on top of them.

Patients travel for elective surgery in a way they don't travel for a checkup, so the service area is a drive-time question rather than a city-limits question. A practice in Rome or Dalton draws from counties in several directions, and the towns inside that radius belong on the site by name so a patient measuring the drive finds their own town on the page.

The Category Call for a Plastic Surgery Practice

Plastic surgeon is the primary category for a board-certified surgical practice, and the certification itself belongs on the profile and in the bio, named by the board that granted it. Google also carries Cosmetic surgeon, Dermatologist and Medical spa, and each of those pulls a different searcher with a different budget and a different question.

The trap shows up when a surgical practice sells injectables and lasers alongside surgery. Filing the whole practice under Medical spa surfaces it for filler and facial searches and quietly drops it out of the surgical ones, which is the wrong trade for a surgeon. Injectables offered inside the surgical practice belong in a secondary slot with a page of their own. A med spa arm operating as a genuinely separate business, its own name, its own staff, its own hours and its own door, is a separate listing question that follows Google's rules for distinct businesses rather than a category preference. What Plastic Surgery Websites Get Wrong

Four problems turn up again and again.

  • One procedures page for everything. Fifteen procedures under a single heading means fifteen searches with no page to land on. Each procedure a surgeon performs earns its own page, with the technique, the candidacy questions, the recovery timeline, and the cost framing on it.
  • The gallery behind a form. Before-and-after images are what patients came for, and a form standing between them and the photos reads as something to hide.
  • Images that crush the page. Galleries built from full-resolution photographs load slowly on the phone most patients are holding, which costs both rankings and consults.
  • A bio with no proof in it. Board certification, fellowship training, hospital affiliations, and how many of a given procedure the surgeon has performed all belong in plain sentences.

Photography carries obligations here that most trades never touch. Before-and-after images have to be actual patients with written consent on file, presented without retouching and without implying a result a typical patient shouldn't expect. Review Dynamics in a Plastic Surgery Practice

The review count grows slowly here, and the reason is plain. Plenty of happy patients have no interest in announcing the procedure under their real name next to their photograph. So this trade never matches the review volume of a plumber, and a practice comparing itself against one is measuring the wrong thing.

Timing helps. The ask works best at the post-operative visit, weeks or months out, once the swelling has gone down and the patient is genuinely pleased. Replies stay general and confirm nothing about who had what done. Third-party procedure review sites carry real weight in cosmetic surgery, so the profile and those sites both get worked, and a patient who prefers to write anonymously somewhere else is still building your case.

Where Plastic Surgeon SEO Starts, and What Follows

The profile and the diagnosis come first: category, secondary categories, hours, photographs of the actual practice, the surgeon's credentials, and a read on where the practice ranks by procedure and by town as things stand. Then the two or three procedure pages tied to the surgeries the practice most wants to fill, with the cost and financing page and the consult path beside them. After that it widens, adding procedure pages and the drive-radius town pages, and the review routine starts at the post-op visit.

The year has a shape worth planning against. Body procedures get scheduled in fall and winter, so the recovery happens under a sweater and the result shows up in June. Facial work clusters in the stretches when somebody can take two quiet weeks off, which in practice means January and the weeks after Labor Day. Consultations run ahead of surgery by a month or more, so the searching that fills a February operating schedule happens in November. The monthly pass gets the procedure pages standing in front of each of those, and the report names which procedures moved and which towns the inquiries came from. Frequently asked questions

Should before-and-after galleries be public or behind a form?

Public. Those images are the single thing a cosmetic patient came to see, and gating them sends people to whoever published theirs openly. Consent and privacy handling still apply in full, and a patient who declines to be shown simply is not in the gallery.

Plastic surgeon or cosmetic surgeon as the Google category?

Plastic surgeon for a practice whose surgeon is board certified in plastic surgery, since that is the closer match to both the credential and the search. Cosmetic surgeon describes a different training path, and choosing a category that overstates a credential creates a problem well beyond search.

Should procedure pages include prices?

Include a starting range and explain what changes it. Cost is one of the most common modifiers attached to procedure searches, and a page that refuses the question loses the reader to one that answers it. Ranges also filter out consults that were never going to close.

How long before a new procedure page starts producing consults?

Procedure plus town searches move first, often inside a few months, because the competition on them is thin. The metro-wide procedure terms take much longer and are usually the wrong first target for a practice that has not yet built out its own county.
