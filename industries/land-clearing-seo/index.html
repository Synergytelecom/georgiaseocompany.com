Two Buyers, One Search Term

The searches look identical and the jobs are not remotely alike. A homeowner wanting a back lot opened up is spending a few thousand dollars and deciding this month. A builder needing a pad cut and a site prepped is spending far more, works to a permit schedule, and is choosing a contractor for a season rather than a Saturday.

Most sites in this trade pick one of those readers by accident, usually the homeowner, because the pictures are of a tidy backyard. The developer lands, sees nothing that looks like their job, and leaves. Separating the two is most of the work: a page for residential lot clearing, a page for site prep and pad work, and the language on each written for the person actually reading it.

Excavating Contractor Is the Category That Carries This

Excavating contractor is the primary that fits, and it is broader than it sounds. It is what Google matches against site prep, grading, and dirt work generally, which is where the larger money sits.

The secondary slots are where the specific jobs get caught. Land clearing service, Demolition contractor, Drainage service, and Grading contractor each pull searches the primary alone will miss, and a contractor doing that work without claiming the slot is invisible for it. Service areas matter more here than for a storefront trade, because the equipment travels and the customer never comes to you.

Photographs Do More Selling Than Copy in This Trade

Nobody hires dirt work off a paragraph. They hire it off a before and after, and this is one of the few trades where the pictures genuinely close the sale on their own. An overgrown, impassable half acre next to the same half acre graded flat and seeded is an argument no sentence improves on.

Equipment photographs do a second job that owners underrate. A buyer with real acreage wants to know whether you own a mulcher or subcontract one, whether the dozer is yours, and whether the machine that shows up will finish the job in the days you promised. A page showing the iron answers a question that otherwise turns into a phone call you might not get.

Clearing Season and the Permit Line

Demand in this trade is not flat across the year. Winter clearing is easier work: the foliage is down, the ground is firmer, and visibility through a tract is better, so the quotes go out faster and the jobs run cleaner. That seasonality should shape when the pages and the profile get pushed, not be discovered in January.

The other thing worth putting on the site is the permitting reality. Land disturbance over a threshold triggers state erosion and sedimentation requirements, and a landowner who has never done this has no idea whether it applies to them. A contractor whose page explains where that line sits, and who handles the paperwork, has answered the question that was actually stopping the call.

Frequently asked questions

What Google category should a land clearing company use?

Excavating contractor as the primary, because it is what Google matches against site prep and grading work generally. Then claim land clearing service, grading contractor, demolition contractor and drainage service in the secondary slots for the jobs the primary misses.

Do photos really matter that much for excavation work?

More than the writing does. Before and after shots of a cleared tract sell this trade on their own, and photographs of the equipment answer the question a buyer with real acreage is actually asking, which is whether you own the machine or have to rent it.

Should the site talk about permits?

Yes. Land disturbance over a threshold triggers state erosion and sediment control requirements, and most landowners have no idea whether that applies to them. Explaining where the line sits, and that you handle the filing, removes the thing that was stopping the call.
