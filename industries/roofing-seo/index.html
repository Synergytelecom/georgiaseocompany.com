Roofing Is a Big Purchase, and Homeowners Shop It That Way

A new roof is one of the biggest purchases a homeowner makes, and they shop it that carefully.

Get a free visibility audit

Fresh Reviews Beat a Big Old Review Count

A homeowner puts a new roof in the same mental category as a new HVAC system or a kitchen remodel, and shops it the same way: reads the reviews closely, checks the dates on them, and gets more than one estimate before signing. That makes how recent your reviews are the biggest trust signal in this trade, ahead of how many you have. A profile whose newest review is three years old reads one of two ways, that the work fell off or that nobody has asked, and both cost the job to a roofer with fewer reviews and newer ones. The fix is not complicated. Ask every homeowner whose roof you just finished, the week you finish it, every time.

The correct primary category is Roofing contractor. Beyond the category, manufacturer certification badges, if the business is a certified installer for a specific shingle brand, insurance and bonding proof, and before-and-after photos of completed jobs, ideally including storm damage work, all carry real weight, since a homeowner letting a crew onto a roof wants proof of both skill and legitimacy before a single quote gets discussed.

The Drone Photos Homeowners Expect

Drone photos have become common enough that not having them is what gets noticed. A homeowner comparing three estimates expects to see their own roof from above, not a stock photo of somebody else's house. If you are already flying a drone to take measurements, the photos are a byproduct of work you do anyway.

When Homeowners Ask About Metal

Metal roofing has grown into an option homeowners specifically ask about during a replacement quote, and a service page that addresses it directly alongside standard shingle work avoids losing that comparison to a specialist competitor by default.

Two Kinds of Demand: Worn-Out Roofs and Hail

Smaller Georgia towns carry a lot of older housing stock working through the tail end of a roof's normal lifespan, which creates steady replacement demand independent of any single storm. Carrollton, a Carroll County city just over 28,300 residents, is one example of a town with plenty of that older housing stock aging toward replacement on its own timeline, on top of whatever storm-driven demand comes through in a given year. The same severe spring and summer storm systems that drive tree service calls across north Georgia also drive roofing calls, hail and wind damage that turns into an insurance claim rather than a scheduled remodel. Both kinds of demand, aging roofs reaching the end of their life and storm damage needing an insurance-driven repair, show up across the same towns our local SEO work already covers, not concentrated in one metro alone. On an insurance job the adjuster's scope of work decides as much as the homeowner does. Write an estimate that lines up with that scope and the job closes. Leave a gap and the homeowner has to go negotiate it, which is where these deals stall. Read the scope before you write the number.

Check Your Inspection Log, Not an Industry Average

In your market, how far apart are a repair and a full replacement? Of the free inspections you ran this month, how many became signed jobs? And of the inspection calls coming in now, how many trace back to a storm or a claim rather than a roof simply wearing out on schedule? No published average answers any of the three. Your inspection log and your calendar do, and they are sitting in the office already.

Frequently asked questions

Why does review recency matter more than total review count for roofing?

Because a roof replacement is a large, infrequent purchase homeowners research carefully. A profile with old reviews reads as inactive even with a high total count, while a smaller but fresher set of reviews signals a business still doing and asking about recent work.

What Google category should a roofing company use?

Roofing contractor is the correct primary category for this trade.

Do storm-driven jobs and scheduled replacements need different marketing?

They pull from different motivations, an insurance claim timeline versus a homeowner's own planning, so photos, service pages, and even review requests can be worded to match which situation actually applies.

How should a roofing business think about a slow month with no signed jobs?

By checking whether it fell during active storm season or not. A quiet month with no storm activity in the area reads very differently than a quiet month in the middle of a busy storm season, and the right response depends on knowing which one happened.
