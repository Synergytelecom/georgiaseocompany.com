What Ecommerce SEO Works On

Ecommerce SEO is the work of getting a store's category and product pages to rank for what shoppers type, then keeping the technical layer clean enough that Google indexes the pages that sell. A catalog produces URLs faster than any other kind of website, so a large part of this job is deciding which addresses deserve to exist at all.

Search demand inside a store splits in two. Somebody typing an exact model number is close to buying and easy to satisfy with a product page. Somebody typing a category, a use, or a problem is earlier in the decision and a much larger audience.

An online store competes statewide and nationally, so the map pack plays a small part here unless you also run a storefront people walk into. When you do, both surfaces belong in scope and the local half gets priced on its own.

Why Category Pages Carry an Ecommerce Store

Collection pages on Shopify, category pages on WooCommerce or BigCommerce, whatever a platform calls them, these are the pages with a real shot at the searches that bring new customers. They gather the products, they can carry copy that answers a shopper's question, and they hold their ranking when one item goes out of stock.

Stores usually treat them as a grid with a heading on top. What they need is whatever the shopper is trying to settle: what separates the options, which size or spec fits which job, what shipping and returns look like, and which products a beginner should start with. Written that way, one category page can outperform the hundred product pages sitting under it.

Product pages have a problem of their own. A description pasted from the supplier feed appears on every other store carrying that item, which gives Google no reason to prefer yours. The answer is rarely rewriting an entire catalog. It's rewriting the products that sell, in batches, starting with the ones already collecting impressions.

Customer reviews on a product page do two jobs at once. They give the page words no competing store has, since your buyers describe the item in their own language, and marked up correctly they can show a star rating in the result. That markup only goes on where the reviews are real and visible on the page.

The Technical Layer Under an Ecommerce Catalog

Here's where the work stops resembling anything a service business needs:

  • Faceted navigation. Filters for color, size, price, and brand can multiply one category into thousands of addresses. Deciding which combinations get indexed, and which get closed off, keeps your own pages from competing with each other.
  • Variants and near-duplicates. One product in eight colors should read as one page, and canonical tags settle which version Google keeps.
  • Out of stock and discontinued. An item coming back in spring keeps its page. An item gone for good redirects to the category that replaced it, so the links and rankings it earned land somewhere useful.
  • Product structured data. Price, availability, and review data marked up so Google can read them, which is the same data feeding free listings in Google Merchant Center.
  • Speed under an app stack. Every app, plugin, and tracking script adds weight. Largest Contentful Paint, Interaction to Next Paint, and Cumulative Layout Shift get measured in PageSpeed Insights before and after anything gets touched.
  • Internal search and pagination. Site search result URLs and page thirty of a category, handled so they stop crowding out the pages you want ranking.

None of this is visible to a shopper, which is why it goes unbought for years. A catalog where Google spends its crawling on filter combinations has less attention left for the category pages you wrote, and the store reads as thin while holding thousands of products.

What Runs Every Month on an Ecommerce Site

A catalog gets its own version of the month. What shoppers are typing across your categories gets read first, with the seasonal swings a store lives on watched right beside it. Search Console gets read for queries collecting impressions and no clicks, which usually marks a page ranking on the edge and one pass short. Categories with buyers behind them and no page yet get built. Product pages get fixed in batches. Buying guides get written and linked down into the collections they belong to.

Seasonal categories get built early on purpose. A page published the week demand peaks has no history behind it, so the pages for a fall or holiday line go up months ahead and get their internal links and their first impressions while the season is still quiet.

The report comes by category, tied to the terms each one targets, with the movement since last month beside it. Rankings, sessions, and revenue rarely move in the same week, so all three sit side by side and nothing gets folded into a single score.

Which Stores Ecommerce SEO Suits, and Which It Doesn't

It's a months-long build, so it suits a store with something to defend: your own products, a genuine specialty, a catalog you control, and margin per order that survives the wait. It suits a store already taking orders more than one launching in three weeks.

Where the money does better somewhere else:

  • A dropshipped catalog. When hundreds of stores list the same items with the same photos and the same supplier copy, those results are already settled and price is the only lever left.
  • Most of the revenue on a marketplace. Amazon and Etsy listings run on their own ranking systems, and optimizing them is a separate discipline that we don't take on.
  • A store that needs sales this month. Shopping ads and paid search put products in front of shoppers the week the card gets charged, and an ads specialist gets there faster than we can.
  • A catalog still being decided. Structure follows the product line, so building category pages for a range you might drop in April wastes the build.

How Ecommerce SEO Connects to the Rest of the Store

A store's rankings sit on decisions made during the build, which is why structure gets settled at the wireframe stage whenever we do both jobs. The URL pattern for categories, where variants live, and how filters behave are cheap to decide early and expensive to change once a designer has started.

Two other things overlap. A rebuild or a platform move puts every ranking address at risk, and carrying those through the switch is its own piece of work, laid out on our website redesign SEO page. And the product data we clean up is the same data an assistant reads when a shopper asks it what to buy, which is the subject of our AI search visibility page.

When you already have a developer, the split gets settled in week one. We write the category copy, set the canonical and indexing rules, decide what happens to a discontinued product, and hand over a specific list of changes. Your developer applies them inside a theme they know, which is faster and safer than us learning it on your store's time.

Frequently asked questions

Which ecommerce platforms do you work on?

The SEO work is largely the same across Shopify, WooCommerce, and BigCommerce: category structure, product data, canonical tags, filter handling, and speed. What changes is how much of that a platform lets you control without a developer.

Does every product need custom written copy?

No, and trying to do all of them at once is how this work stalls out. We start with the products already collecting impressions and the categories carrying the most demand, then work down the catalog in batches.

Is ecommerce SEO worth it when most of our sales come from Amazon?

It depends on whether you want a channel with your name on the deed. A marketplace listing ranks inside a system you don't control and can be switched off without notice. Search traffic to your own store takes longer to build and stays yours once it is built. Marketplace listing optimization is a separate service and we don't offer it.

How long before ecommerce SEO produces orders?

Category pages and technical fixes generally take three to six months to show up in rankings, and revenue follows the rankings rather than arriving with them. A store with an existing catalog and existing traffic moves sooner than a new domain with no history.

Do you handle Google Shopping?

The free listings in Google Merchant Center run on the same product data we clean up, so that work carries straight over. Paid Shopping campaigns are ads, and those belong with an ads specialist.
