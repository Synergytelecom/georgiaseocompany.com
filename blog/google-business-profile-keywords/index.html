How to Add Keywords to a Google Business Profile

Owners go looking for the keyword box and there is no keyword box. The profile carries a handful of fields that do the same job, plus one shortcut that gets listings suspended.

Why You Can't Find a Keyword Field on Your Profile

Search a term like adding keywords to a business listing and you'll get advice written for a system that doesn't exist. A Google Business Profile has no meta keywords tag, no tag cloud, no box where you type the phrases you want to rank for. Google chose not to build one, and the reason is worth understanding: a field owners could fill with anything would tell Google nothing.

What it built is a set of structured fields with rules attached. A category list Google controls. A services list. A service area list. A place for reviews written by other people. And it reads your website, which does have room for words. Each of those is a signal Google can check against something. A free-text keyword box is a signal it can only take your word for.

So the honest translation of the question is this: which fields decide the searches my listing turns up on, and how do I use them properly? That question has answers, and none of them involve a keyword field.

Where to Add Keywords to a Google Business Profile

Your words belong in specific places, in specific forms. Here's the working list, ordered by how much each one moves.

  1. The primary category. The heaviest keyword decision on the listing, and it's a selection from Google's list rather than a phrase you compose. Pick the label matching what customers type, and choosing your categories covers how to find it.
  2. Secondary categories, for lines of work the business actually runs and can document. They widen what you're eligible for and they carry less weight than the primary.
  3. Services, entered one at a time. This is the closest thing to a keyword field on the profile. Name each job the way a customer would ask for it, and use the description under each service to say what it covers.
  4. Products, if you sell things by name. Each entry gets a title and a description.
  5. The business description. Write it for a person deciding whether to call, using the words they'd use. A paragraph of stacked phrases reads as spam to a reader and buys nothing with Google.
  6. Updates. Short posts about jobs you finished and work you're booking. They keep the listing looking current, and current is a signal on its own.
  7. The questions section. Ask and answer the question you field every day, in customer language. Leave it blank and somebody else writes the first answer.
  8. Your website. This is where phrases actually have room to live: a page per service, a page per town, page titles carrying the service and the place.

One item owners expect on that list is missing from it. Photo file names and captions do nothing for position, because the upload strips that information out before Google reads any of it.

Notice the pattern in what's left. The profile takes structured facts and the website takes the prose. Owners who try to force the website's job onto the profile end up with a listing that reads badly and ranks no better.

Keyword Stuffing the Business Name Puts the Listing at Risk

The one field where a phrase can be typed freely is the business name, and that's exactly why it gets abused. A four-word plumbing company name swells into a full line of services and towns, and for a while it works, which is the trap.

Google's rule on that field is narrow: the name has to be the real-world name of the business, the one on your signage, your paperwork, and your invoices. Anything appended for search purposes breaks it. Enforcement comes in three flavors, and the mild one is the least likely.

  • An edit. Google trims the name back, sometimes after a competitor reports it, and you may never get a notification.
  • A hard suspension. The listing disappears from Maps and search, taking the review history offline with it, and reinstatement means an appeal with documents behind it.
  • A reputation problem. Customers read a stuffed name as a business trying too hard, which costs calls in a way no ranking report shows.

Competitors report each other constantly in busy trades, so the businesses running stuffed names in a crowded market are the ones most likely to get caught. A suspension over a name edit costs more than any ranking that name ever bought, and a listing that stops showing up is a repair project rather than an afternoon.

The legitimate version: if your legal or trading name contains a service word, keep it. A business whose real name genuinely carries the trade in it is entitled to that name, and it does get a small honest advantage from it.

Keyword Myths That Cost Local Businesses Map Seats

Four beliefs come up in almost every conversation about profile keywords, and each one has a straighter answer sitting behind it.

The belief: stuffing service words into the profile description will lift the listing.
What holds up: the description barely moves position. It moves whether a reader who found you decides to call, which is worth more than most owners assume and works in a different way.

The belief: reviews mentioning a keyword will rank the listing for it.
What holds up: review text does carry weight, and asking customers to include a phrase is against Google's rules and reads as coached to the next reader. Ask for an honest review and mention the job you did, then let people write their own words.

The belief: more categories means more searches, so add every plausible one.
What holds up: each category dilutes how clearly the listing reads as any one thing, and Google weighs the primary heaviest anyway. A short, accurate list wins.

The belief: what you want to rank for is the input.
What holds up: the inputs are your category, your distance from the searcher, your reviews, how complete the listing is, and the site behind it. Google decides eligibility from those, then sorts. Wanting a search has no field.

Which searches your listing turns up on comes down to a short list of things, and none of them is a phrase you typed into the listing. The category sets what you're eligible for. Proximity narrows the field for each person searching. Reviews, completeness, and activity sort the candidates. The website decides how far you get in the ranked results underneath the map. That's the whole machine, and local SEO is the work of moving each part of it.

Frequently asked questions

Is there anywhere on a Google Business Profile to enter keywords directly?

No field exists for that purpose. The closest equivalent is the services list, where each job you sell gets its own entry and its own short description. Categories, the description, updates, and the website carry the rest of the work a keyword field would do.

Will putting my city and service in the business name help me rank?

It can move a position, and it breaks the rule Google sets for that field, which makes it a poor trade. The name field has to match your real-world name. Enforcement ranges from a silent edit to a suspension that takes your reviews offline while you appeal.

Do the words in my reviews affect which searches I show up for?

Review text does carry some weight. Asking customers to include a specific phrase breaks Google rules, and coached reviews read as coached to the next person shopping you. Ask for an honest review and name the job you completed, then let them write it.

What should go in the business description then?

Plain language aimed at a person deciding whether to call. What you do, where you do it, what a customer should reach out about, and anything that separates you from the next listing. Write it once, keep it current, and expect it to earn calls more than positions.

Where do keywords actually belong if not on the profile?

On your website, in page titles, headings, and body copy, with a page built for each service and each town you work. The profile handles structured facts. The site handles the language, and it is the half that reaches searches a listing cannot.

The Honest Way to Point a Profile at a Search

Pick the one search you most want to own, the exact words a customer would type. Then check whether your primary category is the one Google is answering that search with, whether that service is named on its own line in your services list, and whether a page on your site is built for it. When all three line up, the listing is pointed at that search as directly as the system allows. When they don't, no keyword field would have saved it.
