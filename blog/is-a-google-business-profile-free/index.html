Is a Google Business Profile Free?

By Eric Kirby · Published August 8, 2026

Google charges nothing for the listing. What costs money is getting that listing to show up ahead of the other businesses in your town.

Get a free visibility audit

Yes, a Google Business Profile Is Free to Create and Keep

There's no setup fee, no monthly charge, no ad spend required to be eligible for the map, and no paid tier holding back fields the free version leaves out. Claim the profile, verify it, fill it in, and it stays live at no cost for as long as the business runs.

Verification costs nothing either, whichever method Google assigns you. It might be a code on a postcard, a code read out over the phone, or a video walk through the shop and around the truck.

The performance reporting that arrives afterward is free too, and it names which searches brought people to the listing and how many of them tapped the call button.

Two things reliably convince owners otherwise. The first is a cold call offering to register, activate, or secure the listing for a fee, which is a sales pitch wearing Google's name. The second is an invoice from a marketing vendor for profile work, where an owner reasonably assumes some slice of it passes through to Google.

None of it does. Every dollar spent around a Google Business Profile pays a person to do something with it.

What You Get on a Free Google Business Profile

The free version is the whole product, and every field that decides whether a listing ranks sits inside it.

  • The business name, matched to your signage and your paperwork.
  • One primary category plus additional categories, the pair of fields that draws the boundary around which searches a listing can turn up on.
  • A street address, or the list of towns you drive to. A shop customers walk into publishes the address, and a crew that works at the customer's place publishes the towns.
  • Hours, holiday hours, and a temporarily closed setting for the weeks you go dark.
  • The phone number and the website link, the two buttons a searcher presses.
  • Services and products, entered one at a time, because customers search for the single job they need and a general summary of the business sits outside those searches.
  • Photos and short video of the crew, the equipment, the shop, and finished work.
  • Updates, the short posts that sit on the listing for a while and then drop out of view on their own.
  • Reviews, your replies, and the open questions section that any Google user can add to.
  • Performance data on calls, direction requests, website clicks, and the searches behind them.

What the profile skips is a website. It links out to one and it does not supply one, so the pages a searcher lands on after tapping through are yours to build and host. That's the first place a free listing bumps into a real bill, and what a website costs is published.

What Google Actually Sells Around Your Business Profile

Google earns on advertising, and advertising is a separate product with a separate bill. Ads buy the sponsored slots at the top of a results page, including the unit that can sit above the map cards on a local search. The moment the budget stops, the placement stops with it. The free listing underneath keeps showing either way.

Plenty of paid services get sold alongside all of this, and none of them is a fee for the listing. Review request software. Booking tools that plug into the profile. Photography. An agency retainer. Those are real costs a business can choose, and every one of them is paid to somebody other than Google.

So the honest accounting comes out in three lines. The listing is free. The advertising is optional and metered. The labor is where local search money actually goes, and it goes there whether you spend cash on it or spend your own evenings.

A Free Google Business Profile Still Competes for Three Seats

Free gets a business eligible. Ranked is a separate question, and the difference between the two is arithmetic. Google hands the same free listing to every business in your trade in your town, then shows three of them above the blue links.

In a Rome or Cartersville trade where a dozen listings exist and most were abandoned halfway through setup, the climb is short. It's still a climb.

What separates the three cards Google shows from the rest of the field, on most local searches:

  • A primary category matching the words customers type, which is frequently different from how a business describes itself internally.
  • Service areas drawn where the crew actually goes, since a search made two towns over reads a different field of candidates.
  • A review history that keeps growing, because how recent the reviews are counts alongside the star average.
  • Photos and updates with recent dates on them, which read as a business that is still operating.
  • A website behind the listing naming the same services and the same towns the profile does.
  • Name, address, and phone records that agree across the directories Google cross-checks.

Every one of those is free to do, and none of them stays done. Holiday hours go stale, the service list stops matching what the crew actually sells, the reviews stop arriving the month everybody is buried in work, and a competitor who left half of the setup blank goes back and finishes it. The profile never announces any of that, which is how a listing drifts for months while its owner assumes it's still set up right.

When Paying for Google Business Profile Work Pays Off

An owner with a free afternoon can finish the setup. Claim, verify, pick the category, draw the service areas, name the services, upload real photographs, and start asking for a review while the invoice is still being written. That route is genuinely open, and for a single-location business working one town it's often enough to take a seat.

Paying somebody starts to make sense at specific points:

  • Verification keeps coming back rejected and the notice doesn't say which detail failed.
  • A suspension or a duplicate listing is in play, where a wrong move costs review history that took years to build.
  • You serve six towns and hold a seat in one. Coverage past your own address needs pages, service areas, and directory records working together.
  • The review asking keeps sliding. Everybody intends to ask. The businesses that climb have somebody whose job it is.
  • Nobody's watching the market. A competitor who fixes their category in March takes your seat quietly, and you find out from the phone log.

Frequently asked questions

Does Google charge a monthly fee for a Business Profile?

No. There is no monthly fee, no setup fee, and no paid upgrade that unlocks additional fields. The listing is free to create, free to verify, and free to keep for as long as the business operates.

Somebody called offering to activate my listing for a fee. Is that legitimate?

Treat it as a sales call. Google does not charge to create, verify, activate, or keep a Business Profile, and it does not phone businesses to collect a fee for one. Anybody charging for that work is charging for labor you could do yourself, and the ones using urgency to close are worth hanging up on.

Do I have to run Google Ads for my profile to show up in the map?

No. Advertising and the map pack are separate systems. An ad budget buys sponsored placement while it runs and does nothing to a map pack position in either direction, and a business that has never advertised can hold a map seat.

If the profile is free, what am I actually paying an agency for?

Time and judgment. Picking the primary category that matches how customers search, drawing service areas, writing a page for each town and service, keeping reviews arriving on a schedule, cleaning up directory records that disagree, and watching the market monthly so a competitor closing a gap shows up in a report rather than in your phone log.

Is a Google Business Profile free for a business with no storefront?

Yes. A service business that travels to customers gets the same free listing, hides the street address, and lists the towns it covers as service areas. That setting is the one new profiles most often get backwards, and getting it wrong caps how far the listing reaches.

The Free Part of a Google Business Profile Ends at Setup

Add up what this listing has already cost you over the past year. The photography, the review request software, the ad budget, and whatever a vendor last billed for profile work all belong in that total.

Then open the profile and count the free fields still sitting empty: the categories past the first one, the towns in the service area list, the services named individually, the photos nobody has added since launch. The second number is usually the one keeping you out of the three cards.
