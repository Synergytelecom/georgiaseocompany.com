How to Merge Duplicate Google Business Profiles

Two listings for one business divide every signal the map block runs on. Merging them is a sequence, and the order decides whether the reviews live through it.

How One Business Ends Up With Two Google Profiles

Nobody sets out to create a second listing. It arrives sideways, and by the time somebody notices, it has been collecting customer photos for a year.

The usual origins, roughly in order of how often they turn up:

  • A move. The business relocates, somebody creates a fresh listing at the new address, and the old one stays live with the reviews on it.
  • A vendor. A marketing company or a franchise office set one up years ago on an account nobody can log into now.
  • An employee. Somebody claimed the business from a personal Google account and then left.
  • Google built one. Listings get assembled from other data sources, so an old aggregator record with a retired phone number can produce a second, unclaimed entry on Maps.
  • A rebrand or a sale. The name changed and both names still exist as records.
  • A second phone line or a suite number. One digit of difference is enough for two records to read as two businesses.

The tell almost never comes from Google. It's a customer saying the number they found was disconnected, a review you can't locate in your own dashboard, or a map search where your business appears twice and neither pin looks right.

Hunting Down Every Record Before You Merge Anything

Do the whole inventory before you file anything. Merging two records while a third one sits out there means running this exercise twice, and each pass costs weeks of waiting.

Work through all of these from a private window, since a signed-in search comes back tailored to you:

  1. Search the business name plus each town you've operated in, including any town you left.
  2. Search the street address itself in Google Maps, then read every pin sharing that building or that strip.
  3. Search each phone number the business has ever published, the retired lines included.
  4. Search the pre-rebrand name and any abbreviation you used to use, which is where old records like to hide.
  5. Search your primary category plus the town and read the full Maps list rather than the top three. Unclaimed duplicates often sit well down that list.
  6. Look for a listing that prompts you to claim it. An entry inviting anybody to claim the business is an unclaimed record, and unclaimed records are duplicates in waiting.

Write down what you find in a single list: the name shown, the address, the phone, the review count, whether it's verified, and who appears to control it. That list is the whole decision, and it takes twenty minutes to make.

Deciding Which Duplicate Profile Survives

This is the one irreversible call in the process, so slow down here. The record you keep should be the one carrying the assets you can't rebuild, and the record you retire should be the one carrying information you can retype.

Rank the candidates by what they hold, in this order:

  1. Review history. Count and age both. Years of reviews are the one thing no amount of work recreates.
  2. Verified status. A verified listing you control beats an unverified one with nicer details on it.
  3. Age and standing. An older record with a longer history behind it tends to hold position better than a fresh one.
  4. Access. A listing you can log into is worth more than a listing you're hoping to recover.

Notice what's missing from that ranking: the correct address, the current phone, the good photos, the finished description. All of those move in an afternoon. Reviews never move. Owners talk themselves into keeping the wrong record because it looks tidier, and the tidy one is usually the empty one.

The awkward case is a split, where one record has the reviews and the other has the verification and the access. Keep the reviews and work the access problem, since Google has a process for handing over a listing and no process for handing back a review history.

How to Merge Google Business Profile Records Without Losing Reviews

With the inventory written down and the survivor chosen, the filing is short. The sequence below is what protects the reviews.

Get Control of Both Records First

Claim the duplicate if it's unclaimed. If somebody else manages it, use Google's request-access flow, which notifies the current manager and gives them a window to answer. Silence on their end lets the request move forward. Holding both records turns this into a clean administrative action.

Report the Duplicate From the Record You're Keeping

Google's duplicate flow is the right door. You're telling it that two records describe one business and naming which one is real. A merge folds them together and carries the review history onto the survivor. A deletion discards whatever the removed record held, which is the entire reason to file a merge.

Fix the Survivor While You Wait

The wait runs days to weeks. Correct the address and phone on the record you're keeping, add the services, upload current photos, redraw the service areas, and point the website field at the right page. Leave the retired record alone, since editing both during a pending merge is how the wrong one ends up looking like the more active business.

Confirm the Reviews Landed

Once it completes, count the reviews on the surviving listing against the number you wrote down during the inventory. If any went missing, Google's support channel is where to raise it, and before-and-after counts on paper make that conversation short.

The Second Listing Google Actually Allows

Not every pair of listings is a duplicate, and merging a legitimate one is a self-inflicted wound. Google permits a separate listing for an individual practitioner alongside the business listing, in professions where customers hire a person: attorneys, doctors, dentists, therapists, real estate agents, insurance agents.

The line runs like this. A practitioner listing is allowed when that person is publicly reachable at the location under their own name, and it carries their name as the business name. A practice with four dentists can hold a listing for the practice plus one for each dentist. A solo operator whose own name and practice name describe one thing gets one listing, since two would be two records for the same business.

Other cases that look like duplicates and belong to the same family:

  • A department with its own entrance, staff, and phone number, like a dealership service department separate from sales.
  • Two real locations in the same town, which are two businesses to Google as long as both are staffed and reachable.
  • A storefront plus a service area operation under one name, which is one listing rather than two, and the address question is where that gets decided.

When you're unsure, ask whether a customer standing at each address would find somebody there to help them. Yes at both means two businesses. No at one means a duplicate.

Closing the Source That Made the Duplicate

A merge treats the symptom. Duplicates regrow when the records that spawned them keep feeding old information back into the system, and the second cleanup is more annoying than the first.

So finish the job. Update the address and phone everywhere the business appears: your website footer, Apple Maps, Bing Places, Facebook, the chamber directory, your suppliers' locator pages, the data aggregators that syndicate business records to everybody else. Retire the old phone number from every field it still occupies rather than leaving it as a second line. Consolidate logins so one account you actually control manages the listing, and add a manager rather than sharing a password. Then search the business name once a quarter, in a private window, and read what comes back.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between merging and deleting a duplicate listing?

A merge folds two records into one and carries the review history onto the surviving listing. A deletion removes a record and the reviews attached to it go with it. That is why a duplicate gets reported through the merge flow even when deleting looks faster.

How long does a Google Business Profile merge take to go through?

Days to weeks, depending on whether both records are claimed and whether an access request is involved. Nothing about the wait is worth speeding up by filing the same request twice, which usually resets the clock instead of shortening it.

Somebody else controls the duplicate. What are my options?

Use the request-access flow. Google contacts whoever manages the record and holds the request open for a limited stretch. Let that lapse with no reply and the listing generally comes to you, at which point the merge is ordinary paperwork.

Which listing should I keep when the newer one has better information?

Keep the one with the reviews. Addresses, phone numbers, photos, hours, and descriptions can all be retyped onto the surviving record in one sitting. A review history cannot be moved, rebuilt, or bought back.

Can a law firm or a medical practice keep two listings on purpose?

Yes, in the professions where customers hire a named person. A practice can hold a listing for the business and a separate listing for each practitioner who is publicly reachable there under their own name. A solo operator whose own name and business name describe one thing does not qualify.

Two Listings Split One Business in Half

While a duplicate is live, your reviews sit in two piles, your photos land on whichever record you happened to open, and Google is deciding which version of you to trust. Meanwhile a competitor whose name sits on one record takes the calls your two are splitting, and the only thing visible from inside the business is a phone that rings less.
