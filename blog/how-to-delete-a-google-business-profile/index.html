How to Delete a Google Business Profile

Three different problems send owners hunting for a delete button, and only one of them ends with anything getting deleted.

Three Reasons Owners Want a Google Business Profile Gone

Almost every request to delete a listing traces back to one of three situations, and each one has a different correct move.

  1. There are two listings for one business. A second profile exists, usually created by accident, inherited from a previous owner, or generated by Google from old directory data. This is a merge, and deleting the wrong one throws away reviews.
  2. The location closed. The shop shut down, the business was sold, or you consolidated two offices into one. This is a status change, and marking the listing closed serves customers better than making it disappear.
  3. You want out of managing it. A vendor set it up, or an employee left with the login, or you simply don't want the listing tied to your account anymore. This is an access problem, and it gets solved by moving ownership or removing a user.

Deleting is the right answer for a genuinely duplicate listing you control and never want back, and for a business that closed and left no successor. For every other case there's a better button, which is worth knowing before you press the one that cannot be undone.

How to Remove a Google Business Profile From Your Account

The mechanics are short. Start from the account that manages the listing, search the business name in Google to bring up the management panel, then open the profile settings. Under the settings you'll find the option to remove the business profile, which offers to remove the profile content along with the managers attached to it. Confirm and it's done.

Understand what that button actually does, because owners are routinely surprised.

  • It removes your management and the content you added. Your photos, your posts, your descriptions, your services.
  • The business can stay on Maps. Google builds listings from many sources, so an unclaimed record for the same business often remains after a deletion, still visible, still collecting customer photos, and now completely outside your control.
  • Reviews are the expensive part. A listing that took years to accumulate a review history can lose it. There's no undo, and a fresh listing starts at zero.
  • Nothing about it improves a ranking. An owner deleting a listing hoping to start clean usually recreates the same problem on an account with no history behind it.

Treat this as the last option on the list, reached for well after the others. If the listing is missing from search results and you were headed for delete out of frustration, the causes are worked through at Google Business Profile not showing up, and most of them are repairs.

A Duplicate Google Business Profile Needs a Merge Request

Finding the duplicate comes first, and it takes a private browsing window so your own account stops personalizing what you see. Search the business name plus the city. Then search the old phone number. Then look up the business in Google Maps and check nearby pins for the same name at a previous address or a suite number you stopped using. Old listings hide behind small differences: an abbreviation, a former street, a name from before the rebrand.

Once you've got both listings side by side, decide which one survives. The one with the review history and the verified status is almost always the keeper, even when the other one has better information on it, because information is easy to move and reviews are impossible to move.

Working the Merge

  1. Claim the duplicate if you can. Controlling both records is what lets you resolve them cleanly.
  2. Report it as a duplicate through Google's own flow. A merge folds the two records together and preserves the review history on the surviving listing, where a deletion discards whatever sat on the one you removed.
  3. If somebody else holds the duplicate, request access. Google notifies the current manager and gives them a window to respond before the request moves forward.
  4. Fix what created it. Old directory records carrying the previous address or phone keep feeding the duplicate back into existence, so the citation cleanup is part of the job.

Merges go wrong in one direction in particular. A merge performed against the wrong surviving record can take reviews with it, which is the single most common way an owner loses years of accumulated trust in an afternoon.

What a Duplicate Google Business Profile Costs You in the Map Pack

The damage from a second listing is quiet, which is why it survives so long. Nothing looks broken. Both records show up, both have your name on them, and the business keeps getting calls. Meanwhile the signals that decide map pack position are being split in half.

Reviews divide between the two records, so a business with a solid combined history presents as two weak ones. Photos and posts land on whichever listing you happen to open, so both look half-tended. Directory records and the listing sites Google reads alongside your profile end up pointing at conflicting phone numbers or addresses, which reads as an unsettled business where one clean record would read as an established one. Worst of all, Google has to decide which record to trust, and while it decides, neither one builds the standing it needs to hold a seat.

The practical result is a competitor with one clean, complete listing passing both of yours on searches you should be winning. Owners in that spot often conclude the market got harder. What actually happened is that their own signal got divided. Consolidating to one record, then working that record every month, is the fix, and it's the same order of operations behind the map pack work described on our local SEO page.

Marking a Google Business Profile Permanently Closed

When a location genuinely closes, the listing has a job left to do. Mark it permanently closed and it keeps its reviews, keeps its history, and shows customers a plain closed label so they stop calling a disconnected number and stop driving to an empty building. Delete it and Google may well continue showing an unclaimed version with no closed label at all, which is worse for everybody.

A few variations come up often:

  • You moved. Edit the address on the listing you already have. The review history and the age of the record follow the business to the new address, and both are worth keeping.
  • You consolidated locations. Mark the closed one permanently closed and put the effort into the surviving listing, which now serves those customers.
  • You're closed for a season or a renovation. Temporarily closed is the setting for that, and it holds the listing's standing while you're dark.
  • The business sold. Ownership of the listing transfers with the business. Handing over the profile keeps the reviews with the location the customers actually reviewed.

Closed status can take time to appear, and a stubborn listing sometimes needs the change requested more than once before it holds.

Frequently asked questions

Will deleting my Google Business Profile remove the business from Google Maps?

Often it won't. Google assembles listings from several sources, so an unclaimed record for the same business frequently remains on Maps after a deletion, still visible to customers and no longer editable by you. Marking a closed business permanently closed handles that situation better.

What happens to my reviews if I delete the profile?

You should expect to lose them. Review history is attached to the listing, and a fresh profile starts at zero. That's the main reason a duplicate gets merged rather than deleted.

How do I find out whether I have a duplicate listing?

Search your business name plus your city in a private browsing window, search your old phone number, then look up the business in Google Maps and check nearby pins for the same name at a previous address. Duplicates usually hide behind an abbreviation, an old street, or a former business name.

Can I delete a Google Business Profile somebody else created for my business?

Not directly. You request access through Google, which notifies whoever currently manages the listing and gives them a window to respond. If they do not, the request can move forward and control passes to you.

We moved to a new address. Should the old listing be deleted?

Edit the address on the existing listing instead. The reviews and the age of the record are valuable and they travel with the profile. Creating a second listing at the new address is how duplicates get made in the first place.

Before You Delete a Google Business Profile You Might Want Back

Name the situation first. Two listings means a merge. A closed location means a status change. An access problem means moving a user. Deletion belongs to a short list of cases, and the reviews it takes with it are the part nobody gets back.

Duplicates and suspensions are the two places we watch owners lose the most ground on their own, so if either one describes your listing, hand over the business name and the ground you cover.
