Search Engine Optimization Examples, Line by Line

By Eric Kirby · Published August 9, 2026

Optimizing a page comes down to four edits a person can read, shown before and after on three everyday kinds of page.

What Optimization Actually Changes on a Page

Strip the jargon off on-page SEO and four things get edited. The title tag, the line showing in a search result and in the browser tab. The H1, the visible headline at the top. The body copy, the words a customer reads. The internal links, which of your own pages this one points at and how they're worded.

Everything else is a variation on the four or a technical setting underneath them. So each specimen holds at four lines, before and after, with the reason attached. The placeholders stay generic, so lift the shape and fill it with the words your own customers use.

If any of the vocabulary needs unpacking first, what SEO is in plain English walks through crawling, indexing, and the difference between a ranking and an ad.

A Search Engine Optimization Example, Start to Finish

Specimen One: A Page Selling a Single Service

Take a service page selling water heater repair. Before the work it reads like a page built for a filing cabinet.

Title tag before: Services

Title tag after: Water Heater Repair in [Town], GA | [Business Name]

The before version competes for nothing. "Services" describes a menu position and matches no search anybody runs. The after version names the job and the place, and leaves the business name at the end where it costs the least room. Keep the whole line under about sixty characters or Google trims it.

H1 before: Our Services

H1 after: Water Heater Repair in [Town], GA

The H1 repeats the subject in plainer clothes, dropping the business name and the pipe. One H1 per page, and it should tell whoever just landed that they're in the right place.

Body copy before: "We offer a wide range of quality plumbing services to meet all your needs. Our experienced team is committed to excellence."

Body copy after: "A water heater that runs cold in the morning is usually one of three parts: the thermostat, the heating element, or a tank that has finally rusted through. We test all three on the first visit and quote the work before we start it. Tank swaps are typically same day, and the common sizes ride on the truck."

The before version is a paragraph of adjectives. It could sit on any page for any trade in any town, and it answers no search.

The after version answers what a person with a cold shower actually typed, and it names things that exist: the three parts, the test, what's already on the truck. Specifics separate a page that ranks from a page that fills space.

Internal links before: one "click here" link to the contact page.

Internal links after: a link reading "tankless water heater installation" pointing at that page, one reading "emergency plumbing in [Town]" pointing at the town page, and the contact link reading "get a water heater quote."

Link text is a label Google reads, and "click here" labels nothing. Three descriptive links also keep a reader moving through the site, and they tell Google which of your pages is about what. On-page SEO goes deeper on the fields around these four.

The Same Four Changes on a Town Page

Specimen Two: One Town, Nine Near Copies

A town page fails differently. The usual before state is the home page with one place name swapped in, nine times over, which Google reads as nine versions of one thin page.

Title tag before: [Business Name] | Serving [Town] and Surrounding Areas

Title tag after: Plumber in [Town], GA | Water Heaters and Drain Repair

"Surrounding areas" is a phrase nobody searches. The after version leads with what a person in that town types, then spends the rest of the room on the two services that bring the most calls there.

H1 before: Proudly Serving [Town], Georgia

H1 after: Plumbing Repair in [Town], GA

Body copy before: the home page's three paragraphs with the town name substituted twice.

Body copy after: the county the town sits in, the roads and neighborhoods the crew genuinely runs, the age of the housing stock and what that means for the pipe under it, which of your services this town calls about most, and how long a truck takes to get there.

Those details do two jobs. They're why the page differs from the other eight, and why a reader in that town believes the business has been there. A page about Cartersville that would read identically about Dalton has managed neither.

Internal links before: a footer block listing all thirty town pages on every page of the site.

Internal links after: links to the three or four neighboring towns the crew crosses into, plus the service pages named in the copy above.

Thirty identical footer links tell Google very little. A handful of links between towns that genuinely border each other reads like a service footprint, because it's one.

What a Homepage Example Changes and What It Leaves Alone

Specimen Three: The Page With Two Jobs

The home page is where the four edits get held back on purpose, because it carries a second job: convincing a stranger in five seconds that this is a business worth calling.

Title tag before: Home

Title tag after: [Business Name] | Plumber in [Town], GA

"Home" is the most commonly wasted title tag on small business sites. The after version puts the brand first, reversing the service page order, because the home page is the one people search by name.

H1 before: Quality You Can Trust

H1 after: Plumbing Repair and Water Heater Installation in [Town], GA

A slogan H1 is the hardest edit to sell an owner on, and the one worth insisting about. Say what the business does and where. The slogan keeps its place in the subheading underneath, where it reads better anyway.

Body copy before: a paragraph about the company's commitment to customer satisfaction.

Body copy after: the two or three services bringing in the most work, each in one sentence and each linked to its own page, then the towns covered, then what happens when somebody calls.

Internal links after: the strongest service pages and town pages, worded as the services and towns themselves. A home page passes more weight through its links than any other page, so what it points at matters more here.

What stays untouched: the phone number where a thumb expects it, the reviews, the photographs of real work. Optimization edits the words around those. Clearing them out for copy is how a page ranks better and sells worse.

What These SEO Examples Leave Out

Four edits on one page will move a long, specific search. On a short contested one they move very little alone. Pretending otherwise is how owners end up disappointed by correct work.

Sitting outside these specimens:

  • The map band. Nothing written on a page moves a map card. That contest runs on a listing, and how a Google Business Profile drives local SEO covers the line between the two.
  • Links from other websites. The trust half of a ranked position gets built off your own site, and link building is the slow part of it.
  • Technical state. A page can carry a perfect title tag and still lose if it takes six seconds to load or breaks on a phone. Technical SEO covers that ground.
  • How many pages exist. One optimized page reaches one family of searches, so coverage across a market is arithmetic. That's why keyword research comes before the writing.

Timing sits outside them too. An edited page gets recrawled on Google's schedule, and a long specific search such as water heater repair plus a town name can turn over inside weeks while a two word search takes quarters. How long SEO takes covers both clocks.

Frequently asked questions

Where do I find the title tag on my own page?

Hover the browser tab, or look at how the page appears in a search result. That line is the title tag. In a website builder it usually sits in a page settings panel labeled SEO title or meta title, separate from the page name in your menu, which is why the two so often disagree.

Should the title tag and the H1 say the same thing?

Close, and worded differently. The title tag gets written for a search result, so it carries the town and the business name. The H1 gets written for the person who just landed, so it drops the punctuation and reads like a headline. Making them word for word identical wastes the one line you get in search.

How many search terms should one page target?

One family of them. A page about water heater repair can rank for the repair phrasings, the town versions, and the near me variants, because those are the same request in different clothes. Tankless installation is a different request and earns its own page.

Do these edits work on a drag and drop website builder?

Yes. Every builder worth using exposes the title tag, the headline setting, the body text, and the link text. The fields are buried in different panels from one platform to the next, and what they control is identical.

How soon after editing a page does anything change in search?

Google has to recrawl it first, which runs days on an active site and weeks on a quiet one. After that, a long specific search such as water heater repair plus a town name can move quickly, while a two word search may take a few quarters. Watch impressions in Search Console before you watch positions.

Run These Four Checks on Your Own Best Page

Open the page that matters most to your business and read its title tag in the browser tab. Then the H1. Then read the first paragraph out loud and ask whether it names anything that exists. Then look at what the page links to and how those links are worded. Four checks, no tools, ten minutes.

Most pages fail at least two of them, and an owner can fix all four in an evening. What takes longer is doing it across every service and every town, then keeping those pages level with whatever your market started typing since the last time anybody looked.
