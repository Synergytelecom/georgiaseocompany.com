Nothing Comes Up When You Search Your Own Business Name

You search your business name, or the exact service you offer plus your town, and nothing of yours comes back, in the map or in the regular results. Ranking poorly is a different problem, and a business ranking poorly is at least on the board. A business missing entirely usually has no Google Business Profile yet, or has one that stalled partway through setup.

The Reasons This Usually Happens

  1. No Google Business Profile has ever been created. This is the most common cause by far, especially for a newer business or one that has always relied on word of mouth. There is nothing on Google to find.
  2. A profile was started but never verified. Someone began the setup process, entered a name and address, and stopped before finishing verification. An unverified profile stays out of search results entirely.
  3. The profile exists, but someone else controls it. A past marketing vendor, a former employee, or a previous owner of the business set it up under a login the current business no longer has access to.
  4. A duplicate listing is splitting the identity. An old, auto-generated, or accidentally re-created profile exists alongside the real one, and Google has not decided which one to trust.
  5. The website has no signals pointing to it yet. A brand new site with no links, no citations, and no Business Profile referencing it can sit invisible for a while because nothing has told Google it exists.

How to Claim My Business on Google

  1. Look your business up on Google Maps first, from a private or incognito window. This rules out your own browser showing you a personalized result and tells you whether a profile already exists, unclaimed or not.
  2. If nothing exists, create the profile at google.com/business. Sign in with an account the business itself controls. A personal address or a former employee's login is how access gets lost later.
  3. The name field should match your signage and paperwork word for word. Packing extra keywords into it breaks Google's rules, and it's a common reason a fresh profile gets flagged.
  4. If an unclaimed profile already exists, claim that one. Google's "own this business" flow attaches the existing listing to your account, which keeps the business at a single profile.
  5. Choose the closest matching primary category, not the broadest one. Pick what the business is, then name the services underneath it. The name and category fields are the wrong place to try to cover every possibility.
  6. Decide whether customers come to you or you go to them. A storefront shows its address. A business that works at the customer's location hides the address and lists service areas. That's a separate setting, and it gets missed often.

How to Verify My Business on Google

Claiming and verifying are two steps, and the profile does very little until the second one finishes. Google decides the method rather than you: a postcard to the address, a phone call or text, an email, or a short video showing the premises, your equipment, and proof you manage the business. Video is now the common one for a business with no storefront, and it fails most often because the recording never links the location to the person holding the camera in a single unbroken take.

Verification can take a few days or a few weeks, and the profile stays limited until it clears. Getting the name, category, and address settings right before you start is what keeps a rejection from sending you back to the beginning.

How to Verify Your Business on Google

Verification is the step that puts the listing live, and Google picks the method: a postcard, a phone call, or a video walkthrough, depending on your business type. Complete it promptly and only once.

If an attempt gets rejected, fix exactly what the rejection named before trying again. Resubmitting the same information sends it back through the same review that just refused it, and each failed pass makes the next one harder.

What Happens After You Are Set Up

Being visible and ranking well are two different things. A new profile takes time to settle, usually a couple of weeks, while Google builds confidence in the information. Give it that window. Then stand somewhere in your service area with a phone, search your own services the way a stranger would, and you should appear somewhere, even if not yet at the top. Try it again from the far side of your service area, since map results shift with where the phone is standing.

From there the listing needs tending. Categories shift, competitors fill in their own profiles, and what customers type changes with the season, so a profile that was accurate the day it went live can be behind a season later. That's the part that keeps going once the setup is finished.

If you're live and showing up for your own business name while still invisible for the services you sell, you've moved past setup and into ranking, which our guide to ranking higher on Google covers.

When a Pro Saves You Time

A first-time claim and verification, with no ownership dispute and no duplicate to untangle, is something most owners can do themselves in an afternoon. Where it gets harder is exactly where those steps stop being enough.

Call in help when:

  • Verification keeps getting rejected and you cannot tell why. Each attempt sent back without a clear fix leaves the following review harder to pass.
  • A past vendor or former employee holds the only login. Google's access request process for a disputed listing is slow and easy to get wrong on the first try.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between claiming a listing and creating a new one?

Claiming attaches an existing, unclaimed profile, one Google generated automatically or a previous owner left behind, to an account your business controls. Creating starts from nothing. Checking for an existing listing first matters, since a second, freshly created profile just competes with the one already out there.

We operate out of more than one location. Does each one need its own profile?

Yes, generally. Each physical location gets its own profile with its own address, hours, and reviews, while a business working from a single office but serving several towns lists those towns as service areas on one profile.

Should a home-based business show its address on the profile?

Start from Google's own rule. A business that does not serve customers at its own location has to hide the address and list service areas. Showing the address is only appropriate for the opposite case, a location customers walk into.

What if my business name already shows up when I search, just not in Maps?

That usually means a profile exists somewhere, unclaimed, suspended, or set up under someone else's login. Searching Google Maps from a private window is the fastest way to tell which case applies.

Do we need a page for every service, or is one general profile entry enough?

Every service worth getting found for needs to be named on the profile specifically. One vague entry, or a description that only covers your main service, leaves everything else invisible to those searches.
