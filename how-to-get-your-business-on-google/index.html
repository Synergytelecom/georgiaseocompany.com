How to Get Your Business on Google

If a search for your own business name comes back empty, here is what usually causes that, and the specific steps to fix it yourself first.

Get a free visibility audit

What You're Seeing

You search your business name, or the exact service you offer plus your town, and nothing from your business appears, not in the map, not in the regular results, nothing. Ranking poorly is a different problem. A business that ranks poorly is at least on the board. A business that is missing entirely usually has not been set up on Google at all, or was set up once and stalled partway through. Whether you searched how to get my business on google, claim my business on google, or how to verify my business on google, the causes below are the same handful.

The Reasons This Usually Happens

  1. No Google Business Profile has ever been created. This is the most common cause by far, especially for a newer business or one that has always relied on word of mouth. There is nothing on Google to find.
  2. A profile was started but never verified. Someone began the setup process, entered a name and address, and stopped before finishing verification. An unverified profile does not show up in search results at all.
  3. The profile exists, but someone else controls it. A past marketing vendor, a former employee, or a previous owner of the business set it up under a login the current business no longer has access to.
  4. A duplicate listing is splitting the identity. An old, auto-generated, or accidentally re-created profile exists alongside the real one, and Google has not decided which one to trust.
  5. The website has no signals pointing to it yet. A brand new site with no links, no citations, and no Business Profile referencing it can sit invisible for a while because nothing has told Google it exists.

Do This Yourself: The Setup Steps

  1. Look your business up on Google Maps first, from a private or incognito window. This rules out your own browser showing you a personalized result and tells you whether a profile already exists, unclaimed or not.
  2. If nothing exists, create the profile at google.com/business. Sign in with an account your business controls, not a personal or former employee's login.
  3. The name field should match your signage and paperwork word for word. Packing extra keywords into it breaks Google's rules, and it is a common reason a fresh profile gets flagged.
  4. If an unclaimed profile already exists, claim it instead of creating a new one. Google's "own this business" flow attaches it to your account without creating a competing duplicate.
  5. Choose the closest matching primary category, not the broadest one. Pick what the business is, and name the services underneath it rather than trying to cover every possibility in the name or category field.
  6. Decide whether customers come to you or you go to them. A storefront shows its address. A business that works at the customer's location hides the address and lists service areas instead. That is a separate setting, and it gets missed often.
  7. Complete verification promptly and only once. Postcard, phone, or video, depending on what Google offers for your business type. If an attempt gets rejected, fix exactly what the rejection named before trying again. Resubmitting the same information does not help.

What Happens After You Are Set Up

Being visible and ranking well are two different things. A new profile takes time to settle, in our experience a couple of weeks, while Google builds confidence in the information. Give it that window. Then stand somewhere in your service area with a phone, search your own services the way a stranger would, and you should appear somewhere, even if not yet at the top. Try it again from the far side of your service area, since map results shift with where the phone is standing.

If you are live and showing up for your own business name but still invisible for the actual services you sell, that is no longer a setup problem, it is a ranking problem, and it is covered separately in our guide to ranking higher on Google.

When a Pro Saves You Time

A first-time claim and verification, with no ownership dispute and no duplicate to untangle, is something most owners can do themselves in an afternoon. Where it gets harder is exactly where the steps above stop being enough.

Call in help when:

  • Verification keeps getting rejected and you cannot tell why. Repeated attempts without a clear fix make each following review stricter, not easier.
  • A past vendor or former employee holds the only login. Google's access request process for a disputed listing is slow and easy to get wrong on the first try.
  • Two or more listings for the same business need merging. A bad merge can wipe out reviews built up over years.
  • Several locations have to go live at once. Each one needs its own profile, its own service areas, and its own verification, done consistently rather than one at a time as schedules allow.

Once a profile is live, keeping it that way is ongoing work, covered in more depth on our Google Business Profile management page.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between claiming a listing and creating a new one?

Claiming attaches an existing, unclaimed profile, one Google generated on its own or a previous owner left behind, to an account your business controls. Creating starts from nothing. Checking for an existing listing first matters, since a second, freshly created profile just competes with the one already out there.

We operate out of more than one location. Does each one need its own profile?

Yes, generally. Each physical location gets its own profile with its own address, hours, and reviews, while a business working from a single office but serving several towns instead lists those towns as service areas on one profile rather than creating a listing per town.

Should a home-based business show its address on the profile?

Start from Google's own rule. A business that does not serve customers at its own location has to hide the address and list service areas instead. Showing the address is only appropriate for the opposite case, a location customers walk into.

What if my business name already shows up when I search, just not in Maps?

That usually means a profile exists somewhere, unclaimed, suspended, or set up under someone else's login, rather than nothing existing at all. The private window search in the steps above is the fastest way to tell which case applies.

Do we need a page for every service, or is one general profile entry enough?

Every service worth getting found for needs to be named on the profile specifically. One vague entry, or a description that only covers your main service, leaves everything else invisible to those searches.
