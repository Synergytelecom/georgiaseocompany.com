Why Is My Website Not Showing Up on Google?

One free search splits this into two very different problems, and Google Search Console names the one you have.

Get a free visibility audit

Is It the Website, or Is It the Google Business Profile?

Owners call us and say they're invisible on Google. Then the map block at the top of their own results turns out to show the business fine, calls still coming off it, and what has gone missing is the website in the blue links underneath. That map block, the map pack, runs off the Google Business Profile (the free listing behind the map card) and reads almost nothing from the website. The organic results below it run off the website and read almost nothing from the profile. This page is the website half.

One exclusion first. If your work arrives off a supply house's referral list or a manufacturer's dealer locator, and almost nobody types your service into Google, a missing website is a real problem and still the wrong first purchase. Put the money where the calls already come from.

If the business is missing from the map block itself, Google Business Profile not showing up is the closer diagnosis. If nothing comes back under any search, including your own name, start at how to get your business on Google.

Your Business Name Comes Up and Nothing Else Does

Start with the free sort. Search site:yourdomain.com in Google, using your own domain, and that operator returns the pages Google has indexed from it. If pages come back, this section is yours. If nothing comes back, skip to the next one.

Your business name ranks because almost nobody competes for those exact words. The service plus the town is a different contest, against pages built for that search on purpose. Search a service plus rome ga and the organic winners carry Rome in the title and Rome in the body, so one "Services" page covering Floyd, Bartow, Gordon and Polk counties at once has nothing in it built to beat them.

Photos uploaded at full camera size, and a menu that buries the services more than two clicks deep, drag down a page that otherwise reads fine. Owners ask us whether pausing a Google Ads budget is what dropped the site. It didn't, because ads and organic results are scored in separate systems.

Nothing Comes Back at All

Zero results from that search means Google has never indexed a page from the domain, and in our experience the cause is ordinary.

  • New domain, no links in. Google finds pages by following links from pages it already has, so a site launched last month with nothing pointing at it sits unseen for weeks. No fixed age applies: a clean new site with a submitted sitemap can turn up within days.
  • noindex. One line in the page's own code, <meta name="robots" content="noindex">, asking search engines to keep it out. Development sites carry it on purpose and it survives the launch. In WordPress it's a checkbox: Settings, then Reading, then "Discourage search engines from indexing this site." The same block can ride in the server's headers as an X-Robots-Tag, invisible in the page source.
  • robots.txt. This file at yourdomain.com/robots.txt tells crawlers where they may go, and one line reading Disallow: / closes the whole site. Ours is three lines and public: georgiaseocompany.com/robots.txt.
  • Redirects dropped in a redesign. Rebuilds move pages to new addresses, and the old ones that already ranked have to be redirected with a 301 (a permanent redirect), or Google keeps requesting pages that answer 404 now and the rankings leave with them.

How Do I Check This Myself, Without Paying Anybody?

  1. Run the site:yourdomain.com search signed out of your Google account.
  2. Search the page's own code for the word noindex. In Chrome: right click, View Page Source, Ctrl+F.
  3. Open yourdomain.com/robots.txt in the address bar and look for Disallow: / on a line by itself.
  4. Verify the domain in Google Search Console (Google's free reporting account for site owners).
  5. Paste a page address into URL Inspection, the search bar across the top of Search Console. That screen is decoded below.
  6. Open the Page indexing report and read the reasons listed under the graph. "Excluded by 'noindex' tag", "Blocked by robots.txt", "Discovered - currently not indexed" and "Not found (404)" each name a different problem.
  7. Open Security & Manual Actions. In our experience a manual penalty is rarely the answer here, and that report says outright whether one applies.

All of it is free. Any owner who works down that list ends up holding the same diagnosis we would start from.

What the URL Inspection Panel Says, Line by Line

URL Inspection is where the guessing stops. Paste in a page address and Search Console answers in the same order every time. Here is that panel for a page carrying a leftover noindex.

What Search Console printsWhat it's telling you
URL is not on GoogleThe page is out of the index. The reason sits below it.
Crawl allowed? Yesrobots.txt let Googlebot through, so the block is somewhere further in.
Page fetch: SuccessfulThe server handed the page over. No 404, no timeout, no login wall.
Indexing allowed? No: 'noindex' detected in 'robots' meta tagThe page itself told Google to leave. This is the line that decides it.

Those four lines clear the server and robots.txt, which leaves one tag in one file. Pull the tag, press Request Indexing on that same screen, and the page joins a queue. Google publishes no turnaround for that queue, so anyone quoting you one is guessing.

Cleared the Block and the Site Is Still Nowhere?

Clearing a technical block, or writing the service page that was missing, is work an owner can finish alone once they know which one it is. The harder case is a site that has been live for months, with real content on it, and still nowhere in the results. Closing that gap takes new pages and links earned from outside the site, over months.

Hand it off when one of these is true:

  • The vendor who built the site holds the login and won't release the access to look at any of this.
  • The honest answer is a page for every service and every town you serve, which is a build with a schedule attached.
  • You made every fix above, gave it several weeks, and the search results look identical.
