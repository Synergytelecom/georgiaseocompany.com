Why Isn't My Website Showing Up on Google?

The website itself, not the Google Business Profile, is the subject here. What usually causes a site to stay invisible, and what to check first.

Get a free visibility audit

What's Going On

Your Google Business Profile might be fine, or this might not involve a Business Profile at all: this is specifically about the website. You search for your services and your site never appears, or it only shows up when someone searches your exact business name and nothing else. Either version points to the website itself, not the map pack, and the two problems have different causes and different fixes. Whether you searched why isn't my website showing up on google or why is my website not showing up on google, the diagnosis below applies the same way.

This Is Not the Same as a Google Business Profile Problem

A website that does not rank and a Google Business Profile that does not show up in the map pack are frequently confused, because both feel like "we are invisible on Google," but they are separate systems with separate fixes. The map pack pulls directly from your Business Profile and largely ignores the website. Organic results, the ones this guide is about, pull from the website itself and mostly ignore the profile. It is entirely possible to have a strong map pack presence and an invisible website, or the reverse, and treating the two as one problem tends to send the fix in the wrong direction.

If your business is missing from the map itself, not just from the regular results below it, the more relevant guide is Google Business Profile not showing up. If nothing has been set up on Google at all yet, start with how to get your business on Google instead.

Two Very Different Reasons, and How to Tell Them Apart

Google found it, but nothing on it wins the search. For a business with a real, established site already live, this is the more common version by far. One broad "services" page trying to represent everything the business does has no specific answer for a specific search, so there is nothing to rank. A slow site or a confusing menu can lose the same fight even when the underlying writing is genuinely good, since a visitor, and a crawler, gives up before finding it.

Worth ruling out first, though, is the rarer, more binary alternative, and one free check sorts it out: search site:yourdomain.com in Google, using the real domain. Pages listed, just not for the searches you actually want, confirms the case above. Nothing back at all points somewhere else entirely.

Google never found it. A brand new domain with nothing else on the web linking to it yet is the plain version of this. The less obvious version is self-inflicted: a setting meant to keep an unfinished site private during development, or a rule in the site's robots.txt file, never got switched off at launch, and the site has been quietly walled off from crawlers ever since. A redesign can cause the same thing in reverse, moving pages to new addresses without leaving a trail for search engines that still expect the old ones.

Steps to Try First

  • Run the site:yourdomain.com check and read what it tells you. Zero results confirms a discovery problem; results that just do not include your service pages confirm a relevance problem instead, and the next steps differ depending on which one you have.
  • If it came back empty, check for a leftover noindex tag. Development sites commonly carry a meta tag telling search engines to stay out; view the page's source and search for the word "noindex," then have whoever manages the site remove it if it is still there.
  • Check the site's robots.txt file for a blanket disallow rule. This small file, found at yourdomain.com/robots.txt, can block crawlers from the entire site if a line reading something like "Disallow: /" was left in from testing.
  • Verify the domain in Google Search Console and use URL Inspection. This free tool states plainly, for any specific page, whether Google has it, whether it is blocked, and why, which turns guessing into a direct answer.
  • If it is a relevance problem, build one real page per service, not one page for everything. A page specifically about the service being searched, in the town being searched, is what actually competes for that search.
  • Fix the obvious speed and navigation problems. Large, uncompressed images and a menu that buries your main services more than two clicks deep both push a technically sound page further down the results.

When It's Bigger Than a Quick Fix

Fixing a technical block, like a leftover setting hiding the site, or filling in an obviously missing service page, is doable without outside help once you know to look for it. What is harder to fix alone is a site that has been live for months, has real content, and still is not moving. That usually is not a checklist problem anymore. It is a competitive gap, and closing it takes ongoing work, new pages, earned trust signals, and time, not a single afternoon of fixes.

Bring in outside help for any of these:

  • A past web vendor built the site and will not hand over the login or technical access needed to even diagnose what is wrong.
  • The honest answer, once you look closely, is that the site needs real service and town pages built out, not patched piece by piece.
  • You have made the fixes above, given them real time, several weeks at minimum, and nothing has changed, the point where guessing stops being useful.

Frequently asked questions

Is a manual Google penalty a likely reason my site is missing?

In our experience, rarely. Almost every missing site traces back to an ordinary technical block or a genuinely new domain nothing links to yet, not a penalty. A connected Search Console account states plainly whether a penalty applies, so it is worth ruling out first rather than assuming the worst.

Does a website need to reach a certain age before it can rank for anything?

No fixed age applies, and a brand new site with a clean technical setup and a submitted sitemap can appear in search within days. What actually takes longer is earning trust for competitive terms, which happens through real content and a real track record over time, not through the calendar alone.

If we redesign the site, will it stay findable afterward?

Only if old page addresses that already ranked get redirected to their new locations. Skipping that step is exactly what turns a routine redesign into a sudden visibility drop, since search engines keep trying to serve pages that no longer exist.

Does running paid search ads improve organic rankings?

Starting or stopping an ad budget has no effect at all on where a page lands in the regular results underneath it, because the two systems never touch: a paid ad occupies a purchased, temporary slot, and the organic ranking below it is earned on a completely separate track.

My name ranks but none of my actual services do. What's going on?

A dedicated page for each service is the fix here, not a technical repair, because a specific service term is a real contest against other pages built specifically for it. Your business name faces almost no such competition, which is exactly why it surfaces easily while the rest does not.
