Why Is My Website Not Showing Up on Google?

This is about the website, not the Google Business Profile, and the likeliest causes come first.

Get a free visibility audit

What Is Going On

This page is about the website, not the Google Business Profile. Your profile might be fine, or you might not have one at all. You search for your services and your site never appears, or it only shows up when someone types your exact business name. Either version points to the website itself, not the map pack, and the two have different causes and different fixes. Whether you searched why isn't my website showing up on google or why is my website not showing up on google, the diagnosis below applies the same way.

This Is Not the Same as a Google Business Profile Problem

Both problems feel like "we are invisible on Google," which is why they get confused. They are separate systems with separate fixes.

The map pack pulls directly from your Business Profile and largely ignores the website. Organic results, the ones this guide is about, pull from the website itself and mostly ignore the profile. A business can hold the map pack and still have an invisible website, or the reverse, so treating the two as one problem sends the fix in the wrong direction.

If your business is missing from the map itself, not just from the regular results below it, the more relevant guide is Google Business Profile not showing up. If nothing has been set up on Google at all yet, start with how to get your business on Google instead.

One Free Check, Then Two Very Different Reasons

One free check sorts this into two very different problems. Search site:yourdomain.com in Google, using your real domain. Pages come back, just not for the searches you want? Read the first case below. Nothing comes back at all? Read the second.

Google Found It but Nothing Wins

This is the common version for a site that has been live a while. One broad "services" page trying to represent everything the business does has no specific answer for a specific search, so there is nothing to rank. A slow site or a buried menu loses the same fight no matter how well the page is written, because visitors and crawlers give up before they find it.

Google Never Found It at All

A brand new domain with nothing linking to it yet is the ordinary version of this. The less obvious version is self-inflicted. A setting meant to keep an unfinished site private during development, or a rule in the site's robots.txt file, never got switched off at launch, and the site has been walled off from crawlers ever since. A redesign causes the same thing in reverse, moving pages to new addresses without leaving a trail for search engines that still expect the old ones.

Steps to Try First

  1. Run the site:yourdomain.com check first. Zero results means a discovery problem. Results that leave out your service pages mean a relevance problem, and the steps below split accordingly.
  2. Check for a leftover noindex tag if nothing came back. Development sites commonly carry a meta tag telling search engines to stay out. View the page's source, search for the word "noindex," and have whoever manages the site remove it if it is still there.
  3. Check the site's robots.txt file for a blanket disallow rule. This small file, found at yourdomain.com/robots.txt, can block crawlers from the entire site when a line reading "Disallow: /" gets left in from testing.
  4. Verify the domain in Google Search Console and use URL Inspection. URL Inspection will tell you, for any page you paste in, whether Google has it, whether it is blocked, and why. No more guessing.
  5. If it is a relevance problem, build one page per service, not one page for everything. A page specifically about the service being searched, in the town being searched, is what competes for that search.
  6. Fix the obvious speed and navigation problems. Large, uncompressed images and a menu that buries your main services more than two clicks deep both push a technically sound page further down the results.

When It Is Bigger Than a Quick Fix

Clearing a technical block, or filling in a missing service page, is doable without outside help once you know to look for it. Harder is a site that has been live for months, has content on it, and still is not moving. By then it is usually not a checklist problem. It is a competitive gap, and closing one takes new pages, earned trust signals, and time, not a single afternoon of fixes.

Bring in outside help for any of these:

  • A past web vendor built the site and will not hand over the login or technical access needed to even diagnose what is wrong.
  • Look closely and the answer is that the site needs service and town pages built out, not patched piece by piece.
  • You made the fixes above, gave them several weeks at minimum, and nothing changed. Past that point, guessing stops being useful.

Frequently asked questions

Is a manual Google penalty a likely reason my site is missing?

In our experience, rarely. Almost every missing site traces back to an ordinary technical block or a new domain nothing links to yet, not a penalty. A connected Search Console account will say outright whether a penalty applies, so it is worth ruling out first rather than assuming the worst.

Does a website need to reach a certain age before it can rank for anything?

No fixed age applies, and a brand new site with a clean technical setup and a submitted sitemap can appear in search within days. What takes longer is earning trust for competitive terms, which happens through published work and a track record over time, not through the calendar alone.

If we redesign the site, will it stay findable afterward?

Only if old page addresses that already ranked get redirected to their new locations. Skipping that step is exactly what turns a routine redesign into a sudden visibility drop, since search engines keep trying to serve pages that no longer exist.

Does running paid search ads improve organic rankings?

No. Starting or stopping an ad budget does not move a page in the regular results underneath, because the two systems never touch. A paid ad occupies a purchased, temporary slot. The organic ranking below it is earned on a separate track.

My name ranks but none of my actual services do. What's going on?

A dedicated page for each service is the fix here, not a technical repair, because a specific service term is a contest against other pages built specifically for it. Your business name faces almost no such competition, which is exactly why it surfaces easily while the rest does not.
